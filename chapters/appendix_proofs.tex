\section{Pinsker's inequality}
\label{sec:proof-pinsker}

\Cref{thm:pinsker} in \cref{ch:distances} states that for any distributions $\Pr$ and $\Qr$ on the same sample space,
\[
\TV(\Pr, \Qr) \leq \sqrt{\tfrac12 \KL(\Pr, \Qr)}.
\]
It is there to say that a fit that is good in KL is good for every probability computed from it.
\textbf{Nothing later in the book depends on it}: no theorem, proof or exercise after \cref{ch:distances} uses Pinsker's inequality, and you can skip this section without losing anything.

\optionalproof{Two steps. First prove the inequality for two Bernoulli distributions, by calculus in one variable. Then show that collapsing $\Pr$ and $\Qr$ to `in the event $A^\star$ or not' turns them into two Bernoullis with the same total variation and no larger KL divergence. The second step uses the same tangent-line bound $-\log y \geq 1 - y$ as the proof that KL is nonnegative (\cref{prop:kl-properties}).}

\subsection*{Step 1: two Bernoulli distributions}

\begin{lemma}
\label{lem:pinsker-bern}
For $p, q \in (0, 1)$,
\[
\KL(\Bern(p), \Bern(q)) = p \log\frac{p}{q} + (1 - p)\log\frac{1 - p}{1 - q} \geq 2(p - q)^2.
\]
The inequality also holds for $p \in [0, 1]$ and $q \in (0, 1)$, with the convention $0 \log 0 = 0$.
\end{lemma}

\optionalproof{Fix $p$, treat the gap between the two sides as a function of $q$, and show by its derivative that it is smallest, and equal to $0$, at $q = p$.}
\begin{proof}
Fix $p$ and look at the gap as a function of $q$:
\[
g(q) = p \log\frac{p}{q} + (1 - p)\log\frac{1 - p}{1 - q} - 2(p - q)^2.
\]
At $q = p$ both terms are $0$, so $g(p) = 0$.
Differentiate in $q$:
\[
g'(q) = -\frac{p}{q} + \frac{1 - p}{1 - q} + 4(p - q).
\]
The first two terms combine over the common denominator $q(1 - q)$: $-p(1 - q) + (1 - p)q = q - p$. So
\[
g'(q) = \frac{q - p}{q(1 - q)} - 4(q - p) = (q - p)\left( \frac{1}{q(1 - q)} - 4 \right).
\]
The product $q(1 - q)$ is at most $\frac14$, since $\frac14 - q(1 - q) = (q - \frac12)^2 \geq 0$ (it is the variance of one Bernoulli observation, largest for a fair coin).
So $1/(q(1 - q)) \geq 4$ and the bracket is never negative.
The sign of $g'(q)$ is therefore the sign of $q - p$: $g$ decreases until $q = p$ and increases after.
Its smallest value is $g(p) = 0$, so $g(q) \geq 0$ for every $q$.
\end{proof}

\Cref{fig:pinsker} draws the lemma, for $p = 0.645$ on the left.
The divergence curve sits above the parabola $2(p - q)^2$ and touches it only at $q = p$.
Near $q = p$ the two are close: the divergence is approximately $(p - q)^2/(2p(1 - p))$ (\cref{sec:dist-kl}), and $1/(2p(1-p)) \geq 2$, with equality only for $p = \frac12$.
The parabola $2(p-q)^2$ is the divergence's own parabola for the hardest case, the fair coin, and every other Bernoulli curves up faster.
The right of the figure draws the gap $g(q)$ for $p = 0.2$, $0.5$ and $0.645$: each is $0$ at $q = p$ and positive everywhere else.

\centering

\caption{The Bernoulli case of Pinsker's inequality. Left: $\KL(\Bern(p), \Bern(q))$ (blue) and $2(p - q)^2$ (orange, dashed) for $p = 0.645$. Right: the gap between the two, as a function of $q$, for $p = 0.2$ (aqua, dash-dot), $0.5$ (gray, dotted) and $0.645$ (blue, solid).}
\label{fig:pinsker}
\end{figure}

For the kiss data, $p = 80/124$ and $q = \frac12$: $\KL = 0.0428$ and $2(p - q)^2 = 2 \times 0.1452^2 = 0.0422$.
Taking square roots, $\abs{p - q} = 0.1452 \leq \sqrt{0.0428/2} = 0.1462$.
Since $\TV(\Bern(p), \Bern(q)) = \abs{p - q}$, the lemma is Pinsker's inequality for Bernoulli distributions.

\subsection*{Step 2: reducing any pair of distributions to two Bernoullis}

\begin{lemma}[The log-sum inequality]
\label{lem:log-sum}
For nonnegative numbers $a_1, \ldots, a_k$ and nonnegative numbers $b_1, \ldots, b_k$ with $b_i > 0$ whenever $a_i > 0$, and with $a = \sum_i a_i > 0$ and $b = \sum_i b_i$,
\[
\sum_{i=1}^k a_i \log\frac{a_i}{b_i} \geq a \log\frac{a}{b},
\]
with the convention $0 \log 0 = 0$.
The same holds with the sums replaced by integrals over a region.
\end{lemma}

\optionalproof{Rewrite the difference of the two sides as a weighted average of $-\log$ of ratios and apply the tangent-line bound $-\log y \geq 1 - y$ to each.}
\begin{proof}
Only the terms with $a_i > 0$ contribute on the left. For those, write
\[
\sum_{i: a_i > 0} a_i \log\frac{a_i}{b_i} - a\log\frac{a}{b} = \sum_{i: a_i > 0} a_i \log\left( \frac{a_i}{b_i} \cdot \frac{b}{a} \right) = \sum_{i: a_i > 0} a_i \left[ -\log\left( \frac{b_i}{a_i} \cdot \frac{a}{b} \right) \right],
\]
using $\sum_{i: a_i > 0} a_i = a$ to bring $a\log(a/b)$ inside the sum.
Apply $-\log y \geq 1 - y$ to each bracket:
\[
\geq \sum_{i: a_i > 0} a_i \left( 1 - \frac{b_i}{a_i}\cdot\frac{a}{b} \right) = a - \frac{a}{b}\sum_{i: a_i > 0} b_i \geq a - \frac{a}{b} \cdot b = 0.
\]
The last step uses that the $b_i$ with $a_i > 0$ add up to at most $b$.
For integrals the same lines go through with $\int$ in place of $\sum$.
\end{proof}

In words: lumping several outcomes into one can only lower the divergence.
If you record only whether an outcome fell in a set, and not which outcome it was, the evidence it carries cannot go up.

\optionalproof{Collapse both distributions to the indicator of $A^\star$: total variation is unchanged, KL can only fall (\cref{lem:log-sum}), and for the two resulting Bernoullis \cref{lem:pinsker-bern} applies.}
\begin{proof}[Proof of \cref{thm:pinsker}]
If $\KL(\Pr, \Qr) = +\infty$ there is nothing to prove, so assume it is finite; then $q(x) > 0$ wherever $p(x) > 0$.
Let $A^\star = \{x : p(x) > q(x)\}$, and write $a = \Pr(A^\star)$, $b = \Qr(A^\star)$.
By \cref{prop:tv-area}, $\TV(\Pr, \Qr) = a - b$. If $a = b$, $\TV = 0$ and there is nothing to prove, so assume $a > b$; then $0 < a$ and $b < 1$.

Split the sum (or integral) defining $\KL(\Pr, \Qr)$ into the part over $A^\star$ and the part over its complement, and apply \cref{lem:log-sum} to each part, with $a_i = p(x)$ and $b_i = q(x)$:
\[
\KL(\Pr, \Qr) = \sum_{x \in A^\star} p(x)\log\frac{p(x)}{q(x)} + \sum_{x \notin A^\star} p(x)\log\frac{p(x)}{q(x)} \geq a\log\frac{a}{b} + (1 - a)\log\frac{1 - a}{1 - b}.
\]
(If a part has total $\Pr$-probability $0$, it contributes $0$ on both sides.)
The right-hand side is $\KL(\Bern(a), \Bern(b))$, the divergence between the two distributions of the indicator $\ind(X \in A^\star)$.
By \cref{lem:pinsker-bern} (in the extended form for $a \in [0, 1]$; if $b = 0$ the divergence on the right is $+\infty$, which contradicts the finite left side, so $b \in (0, 1)$),
\[
\KL(\Pr, \Qr) \geq \KL(\Bern(a), \Bern(b)) \geq 2(a - b)^2 = 2\,\TV(\Pr, \Qr)^2.
\]
Divide by $2$ and take square roots.
\end{proof}

\begin{intuition}[why the proof works]
Total variation is decided by one event, $A^\star$, where $\Pr$ beats $\Qr$.
Throw away everything about an outcome except whether it fell in $A^\star$.
That leaves a coin, with probability $a$ under $\Pr$ and $b$ under $\Qr$, and the same total variation $a - b$.
Lumping can only lower KL, so the coin's KL is at most the original one.
And for coins, the lemma says KL is at least twice the squared total variation.
\end{intuition}

\section{Each step of the EM algorithm raises the likelihood}
\label{sec:proof-em}

\Cref{sec:lik-em} in \cref{ch:likelihood} states that one E step followed by one M step never lowers the log-likelihood of a mixture.
It explains the climbing curves of \cref{fig:emfaithful}.
\textbf{Nothing later in the book depends on it.}

\optionalproof{Split the log-likelihood of the observed data into two pieces: the quantity the M step maximizes, minus a term that measures how well the parameter predicts the hidden labels. The M step raises the first piece. The second piece can only fall away from its value at the current parameter, by an amount that is a KL divergence and so is never negative.}

\begin{proof}
Write $\theta$ for all five parameters, $\theta_t$ for the current values and $\theta_{t+1}$ for the values after one E step and one M step.
For observation $i$, with hidden label $z \in \{0, 1\}$, let $p_\theta(x_i, z)$ be the joint probability (density in $x_i$) of the observation and the label, $p_\theta(x_i) = \sum_z p_\theta(x_i, z)$ the mixture density, and $p_\theta(z \mid x_i) = p_\theta(x_i, z)/p_\theta(x_i)$ the conditional probability of the label.
Write $q_i(z) = p_{\theta_t}(z \mid x_i)$ for the current weights: $q_i(1) = w_i$ and $q_i(0) = 1 - w_i$.

For any $\theta$ and each $z$, $\log p_\theta(x_i) = \log p_\theta(x_i, z) - \log p_\theta(z \mid x_i)$. Average this over $z$ with the weights $q_i(z)$, which add up to $1$, and add over $i$:
\[
\ell_n(\theta) = \underbrace{\sum_{i=1}^n\sum_z q_i(z)\log p_\theta(x_i, z)}_{Q(\theta)} \; - \; \underbrace{\sum_{i=1}^n\sum_z q_i(z)\log p_\theta(z \mid x_i)}_{H(\theta)}.
\]
$Q(\theta)$ is the complete-data log-likelihood with each label replaced by its weight, which is exactly what the M step maximizes over all five parameters (\cref{ex:lik-em-mstep}), so $Q(\theta_{t+1}) \geq Q(\theta_t)$.
This holds for the sum over $i$, not observation by observation: a step can lower the density of some observations while raising the total.
For the second term,
\[
H(\theta_t) - H(\theta) = \sum_{i=1}^n \sum_z q_i(z)\log\frac{p_{\theta_t}(z \mid x_i)}{p_\theta(z \mid x_i)} = \sum_{i=1}^n \KL\big(p_{\theta_t}(\cdot \mid x_i),\, p_\theta(\cdot \mid x_i)\big) \geq 0
\]
by \cref{prop:kl-properties}, term by term, since $q_i = p_{\theta_t}(\cdot \mid x_i)$.
So $H(\theta_{t+1}) \leq H(\theta_t)$, and
\[
\ell_n(\theta_{t+1}) - \ell_n(\theta_t) = \big(Q(\theta_{t+1}) - Q(\theta_t)\big) + \big(H(\theta_t) - H(\theta_{t+1})\big) \geq 0.
\]
\end{proof}

Ascent says nothing about where the climb ends: EM stops where the gradient of $\ell_n$ is $0$, which can be a local maximum or a saddle point.

\section{The uniform law of large numbers}
\label{sec:proof-ulln}

The consistency proof of \cref{thm:mle-consistent} in \cref{ch:mle-properties} needs the Law of Large Numbers to hold for all candidates $\theta$ at once, not one at a time.
That is the one step of that chapter not proved there.
\textbf{Nothing later in the book depends on this section} beyond \cref{thm:mle-consistent} itself; the asymptotic normality theorem uses consistency, not the uniform law directly.

\recall{The Law of Large Numbers (\cref{thm:wlln}): for i.i.d.\ $X_1, X_2, \ldots$ and a function $h$ with $\var(h(X)) < \infty$, $\frac1n\sum_{i=1}^n h(X_i) \convp \E[h(X)]$.}

\begin{theorem}[Uniform law of large numbers]
\label{thm:ulln}
Let $\Xton$ be i.i.d.\ and let $g(x, \theta)$ be a function of an observation and a parameter $\theta \in \Theta$, with $\Theta \subset \real^d$ bounded and closed.
Suppose that $g(x, \theta)$ is continuous in $\theta$ for every $x$, and that there is an envelope $G(x) \geq \sup_{\theta}\abs{g(x, \theta)}$ with $\E[G(X)^2] < \infty$.
Then, with $M(\theta) = \E[g(X, \theta)]$,
\[
\sup_{\theta \in \Theta}\,\Big|\frac1n\sum_{i=1}^n g(X_i, \theta) - M(\theta)\Big| \convp 0.
\]
\end{theorem}

\optionalproof{Cover $\Theta$ by finitely many small balls. On each ball the function $g$ varies little, so the average over the ball's center controls the average everywhere in the ball; the ordinary LLN handles the finitely many centers at once, and continuity plus the envelope make the within-ball variation as small as you like.}
\begin{proof}
Fix $\epsilon > 0$.
For $\theta \in \Theta$ and $r > 0$ let $B(\theta, r)$ be the ball of radius $r$ around $\theta$, and define the local oscillation
\[
w_r(x, \theta) = \sup_{\theta' \in B(\theta, r) \cap \Theta}\abs{g(x, \theta') - g(x, \theta)}.
\]
For each fixed $x$ and $\theta$, continuity of $g$ in $\theta$ gives $w_r(x, \theta) \to 0$ as $r \to 0$, and $0 \leq w_r(x, \theta) \leq 2G(x)$.
So by dominated convergence \toolkit{sec:math-limits} (the dominating function is $2G$, integrable), $\E[w_r(X, \theta)] \to 0$ as $r \to 0$, for each $\theta$.

A bounded closed set has the following property: for any way of attaching a ball $B(\theta, r_\theta)$ to each of its points, finitely many of those balls already cover it.
Choose $r_\theta$ for each $\theta$ so small that $\E[w_{r_\theta}(X, \theta)] < \epsilon$, and pick a finite cover $B(\theta_1, r_1), \ldots, B(\theta_K, r_K)$.

Now take any $\theta \in \Theta$, say in the ball around $\theta_k$. Split the error at $\theta$ into three pieces:
\[
\begin{aligned}
\Big|\frac1n\sum_i g(X_i, \theta) - M(\theta)\Big| &\leq \underbrace{\frac1n\sum_i \abs{g(X_i, \theta) - g(X_i, \theta_k)}}_{\leq\, \frac1n\sum_i w_{r_k}(X_i, \theta_k)} + \underbrace{\Big|\frac1n\sum_i g(X_i, \theta_k) - M(\theta_k)\Big|}_{\text{LLN at } \theta_k}\\
&\quad + \underbrace{\abs{M(\theta_k) - M(\theta)}}_{\leq\, \E[w_{r_k}(X, \theta_k)] < \epsilon}.
\end{aligned}
\]
Taking the supremum over $\theta$ amounts to taking the maximum over the finitely many $k$, so
\[
\sup_\theta\Big|\frac1n\sum_i g(X_i, \theta) - M(\theta)\Big| \leq \max_{k \leq K}\frac1n\sum_i w_{r_k}(X_i, \theta_k) + \max_{k \leq K}\Big|\frac1n\sum_i g(X_i, \theta_k) - M(\theta_k)\Big| + \epsilon.
\]
Each of the $2K$ averages on the right is an ordinary average of i.i.d.\ terms with finite variance (bounded by $2G$ or $G$, with $\E[G^2] < \infty$), so by the LLN the first maximum converges in probability to $\max_k \E[w_{r_k}(X, \theta_k)] < \epsilon$ and the second to $0$.
A maximum of finitely many sequences converging in probability converges in probability to the maximum of the limits.
Hence with probability tending to $1$ the right side is below $3\epsilon$, and since $\epsilon$ was arbitrary, the left side converges in probability to $0$.
\end{proof}

The theorem is applied in \cref{thm:mle-consistent} with $g(x, \theta) = \log p_\theta(x)$, where the envelope $G = \sup_\theta\abs{\log p_\theta(X)}$ is the hypothesis stated there, with the finite second moment the book's version of the Law of Large Numbers needs.

\section{The densities of the chi-squared and Student distributions}
\label{sec:proof-chisq-density}

\Cref{prop:chisq-props}(3) in \cref{ch:t-test} states the density of $\chi^2_k$, and \cref{prop:student-props}(2) the density of $t_k$.
The chapter proves the $k = 1$ case of the first, and afterwards uses the chi-squared density only in \cref{ex:t-variance}, for the variance of $t_k$: its tests and intervals need only the definitions of the two distributions as a sum of squares and as a ratio, and their quantiles.
\textbf{Nothing later in the book depends on this section.}

\begin{proposition}
For $k \geq 1$, the density of $\chi^2_k$ is $f_k(v) = \dfrac{1}{2^{k/2}\Gamma(k/2)}v^{k/2 - 1}e^{-v/2}$ for $v > 0$.
\end{proposition}
\optionalproof{Induction on $k$: adding one more squared Gaussian convolves $f_k$ with $f_1$, and the convolution integral is a Beta integral.}
\begin{proof}
The case $k = 1$ is in the chapter.
Suppose the formula holds for $k$, and let $V \sim \chi^2_k$ and $Z^2 \sim \chi^2_1$ be independent, so $V + Z^2 \sim \chi^2_{k+1}$.
The density of a sum of independent variables is the convolution of the densities: for $x > 0$,
\[
f_{k+1}(x) = \int_0^x f_k(u)\,f_1(x - u)\,\mathrm{d}u = \frac{1}{2^{k/2}\Gamma(k/2)}\cdot\frac{1}{2^{1/2}\Gamma(1/2)}\,e^{-x/2}\int_0^x u^{k/2 - 1}(x - u)^{-1/2}\,\mathrm{d}u,
\]
since $e^{-u/2}e^{-(x-u)/2} = e^{-x/2}$ comes out of the integral.
Substituting $u = xs$,
\[
\int_0^x u^{k/2 - 1}(x - u)^{-1/2}\,\mathrm{d}u = x^{(k+1)/2 - 1}\int_0^1 s^{k/2 - 1}(1 - s)^{-1/2}\,\mathrm{d}s = x^{(k+1)/2 - 1}\,\frac{\Gamma(k/2)\,\Gamma(1/2)}{\Gamma((k+1)/2)},
\]
where the last step is the Beta integral $\int_0^1 s^{a-1}(1-s)^{b-1}\,\mathrm{d}s = \Gamma(a)\Gamma(b)/\Gamma(a+b)$, a standard identity of calculus.
The Gamma factors cancel against those in front, leaving
\[
f_{k+1}(x) = \frac{1}{2^{(k+1)/2}\,\Gamma\big(\frac{k+1}{2}\big)}\,x^{(k+1)/2 - 1}e^{-x/2},
\]
the formula for $k + 1$.
\end{proof}

\begin{proposition}
For $k \geq 1$, the density of $t_k$ is $g_k(t) = \dfrac{\Gamma\big(\frac{k+1}{2}\big)}{\sqrt{k\pi}\,\Gamma\big(\frac{k}{2}\big)}\Big(1 + \dfrac{t^2}{k}\Big)^{-(k+1)/2}$.
For $k = 1$ it is the Cauchy density $1/(\pi(1 + t^2))$.
\end{proposition}
\optionalproof{Condition on the denominator: given $V = v$, the ratio is a Gaussian scaled by $\sqrt{k/v}$. Integrate the resulting Gaussian density against the density of $V$; the integral is a Gamma integral.}
\begin{proof}
Let $T = Z/\sqrt{V/k}$ with $Z \sim \Gauss(0,1)$ and $V \sim \chi^2_k$ independent, and let $\varphi$ and $\Phi$ be the standard Gaussian density and CDF.
Since $V > 0$, $\{T \leq t\} = \{Z \leq t\sqrt{V/k}\}$, and by independence, conditioning on $V$,
\[
\Pr(T \leq t) = \int_0^\infty \Phi\big(t\sqrt{v/k}\big)\,f_k(v)\,\mathrm{d}v.
\]
Differentiating in $t$ under the integral (the integrand's derivative, $\varphi(t\sqrt{v/k})\sqrt{v/k}\,f_k(v)$, is bounded by $\sqrt{v/k}f_k(v)$, which is integrable),
\begin{align*}
g_k(t) &= \int_0^\infty \frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}e^{-t^2 v/(2k)}\sqrt{\frac{v}{k}}\cdot\frac{v^{k/2-1}e^{-v/2}}{2^{k/2}\Gamma(k/2)}\,\mathrm{d}v \\
&= \frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi k}\,2^{k/2}\Gamma(k/2)}\int_0^\infty v^{\frac{k+1}{2} - 1}\exp\Big(-\frac{v}{2}\Big(1 + \frac{t^2}{k}\Big)\Big)\mathrm{d}v.
\end{align*}
The integral is $\int_0^\infty v^{a-1}e^{-bv}\,\mathrm{d}v = \Gamma(a)/b^a$ with $a = (k+1)/2$ and $b = \frac12(1 + t^2/k)$, that is, $\Gamma\big(\frac{k+1}{2}\big)\,2^{(k+1)/2}(1 + t^2/k)^{-(k+1)/2}$.
Collecting the powers of $2$, $2^{-1/2}\cdot 2^{-k/2}\cdot 2^{(k+1)/2} = 1$, gives the formula.
For $k = 1$, $\Gamma(1) = 1$ and $\Gamma(\frac12) = \sqrt{\pi}$, so $g_1(t) = 1/(\pi(1 + t^2))$.
\end{proof}

\section{The Benjamini--Hochberg procedure controls the false discovery rate}
\label{sec:proof-bh}

\Cref{thm:bh} in \cref{ch:t-test} states that the Benjamini--Hochberg procedure has $\mathrm{FDR} = (N_0/N)\alpha$ when the $p$-values of the true nulls are uniform on $[0,1]$ and independent of each other and of the remaining $p$-values.
\textbf{Nothing later in the book depends on this section.}

Recall the procedure: with $P_{(1)} \leq \cdots \leq P_{(N)}$ the sorted $p$-values, $i_{\max} = \max\{i : P_{(i)} \leq i\alpha/N\}$ ($0$ if the set is empty), and the tests with the $i_{\max}$ smallest $p$-values are rejected.
Write $R = i_{\max}$ for the number of rejections and $V$ for the number of rejected true nulls.
Two observations about the procedure are the heart of the proof.

\begin{lemma}
\label{lem:bh-monotone}
(a) Lowering any one $p$-value cannot decrease $R$.
(b) Fix a test $i$ and let $R^{(i)}$ be the number of rejections when $P_i$ is replaced by $0$ and the others are unchanged. Then test $i$ is rejected if and only if $P_i \leq \alpha R^{(i)}/N$, and on that event $R = R^{(i)}$.
\end{lemma}
\optionalproof{(a) Sorted values move down when any value does. (b) Zeroing a rejected $p$-value shuffles only the ranks below it, so the count of rejections is unchanged; conversely, if $P_i$ is under the line set by the zeroed vector, enough values are under it in the original vector too.}
\begin{proof}
(a) If one $p$-value is lowered, every sorted value $P_{(j)}$ can only go down, so every index $j$ with $P_{(j)} \leq j\alpha/N$ before still satisfies it after, and the largest such index cannot fall.

(b) Write $P'$ for the modified vector (with $P'_i = 0$) and $R' = R^{(i)}$.
By (a), $R' \geq R$.
Suppose first that test $i$ is rejected, so its rank is at most $R$ and $P_i \leq P_{(R)} \leq R\alpha/N$.
Moving $P_i$ to $0$ changes the sorted vector only at ranks up to the rank of $P_i$, which is at most $R$; for $j > R$, $P'_{(j)} = P_{(j)} > j\alpha/N$ by the maximality of $R$, so $R' \leq R$.
Hence $R' = R$ and $P_i \leq \alpha R'/N$.
Conversely, suppose $P_i \leq \alpha R'/N$.
Under $P'$ the $R'$ smallest values are rejected and $P'_i = 0$ is among them; the other $R' - 1$ rejected values are unchanged original $p$-values, each at most $P'_{(R')} \leq R'\alpha/N$.
Together with $P_i$ itself, at least $R'$ of the original $p$-values are at most $R'\alpha/N$, so $P_{(R')} \leq R'\alpha/N$ and $R \geq R'$; with (a), $R = R'$.
Finally, test $i$ has rank at most $R$: otherwise $P_i \geq P_{(R+1)} > (R+1)\alpha/N > R\alpha/N = R'\alpha/N$, contradicting the assumption.
So test $i$ is rejected.
\end{proof}

\optionalproof{Write the false discovery proportion as a sum over the true nulls, replace $R$ by $R^{(i)}$ using the lemma, and condition on the other $p$-values: the uniform $P_i$ falls under its threshold with probability exactly $\alpha R^{(i)}/N$, and the $R^{(i)}$ cancels.}
\begin{proof}[Proof of \cref{thm:bh}]
On the event that nothing is rejected, $V/\max(R,1) = 0$; otherwise $R \geq 1$ and $V/R$ counts the rejected true nulls.
So, summing over the true nulls $i$ and using \cref{lem:bh-monotone}(b) to rewrite each indicator and to replace $R$ by $R^{(i)}$ on the event,
\[
\mathrm{FDR} = \E\Big[\frac{V}{\max(R,1)}\Big] = \sum_{i \text{ true null}} \E\Big[\frac{\ind(P_i \leq \alpha R^{(i)}/N)}{R^{(i)}}\Big].
\]
Note that $R^{(i)} \geq 1$ always, since the modified vector has a $p$-value equal to $0 \leq \alpha/N$.
The number $R^{(i)}$ is a function of the other $p$-values only (with $P_i$ replaced by the constant $0$), so by independence, conditioning on them and using that $P_i$ is uniform,
\[
\E\Big[\frac{\ind(P_i \leq \alpha R^{(i)}/N)}{R^{(i)}}\Big] = \E\Big[\frac{\Pr(P_i \leq \alpha R^{(i)}/N \mid \text{others})}{R^{(i)}}\Big] = \E\Big[\frac{\alpha R^{(i)}/N}{R^{(i)}}\Big] = \frac{\alpha}{N},
\]
where $\alpha R^{(i)}/N \leq \alpha \leq 1$ keeps the uniform probability equal to its argument.
Summing over the $N_0$ true nulls gives $\mathrm{FDR} = N_0\alpha/N$.
\end{proof}

Without independence the identity can fail; Benjamini and Yekutieli (2001) proved, by an argument not reproduced here, the general bound $\mathrm{FDR} \leq (N_0/N)\,\alpha\,(1 + \frac12 + \cdots + \frac1N)$, which is why the procedure is sometimes run at the level $\alpha/(1 + \frac12 + \cdots + \frac1N)$ when the tests are arbitrarily dependent.
