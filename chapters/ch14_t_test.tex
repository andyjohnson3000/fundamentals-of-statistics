\chapter[The $t$-Test]{Chi-Squared, Student's $t$, Cochran's Theorem and the $t$-Test}
\label{ch:t-test}

\begin{chaptergoals}
\begin{itemize}[nosep]
\item Why a small Gaussian sample needs its own theory: the standardized mean with an estimated standard deviation is not Gaussian, and at $n = 5$ the Gaussian $5\%$ rule rejects a true null $12\%$ of the time.
\item The chi-squared distribution: a sum of $k$ squared standard Gaussians, with mean $k$ and variance $2k$. It is the distribution of the sample variance and, in \cref{ch:wald-lrt}, of every squared test statistic.
\item Cochran's theorem: for Gaussian data the sample mean and the sample variance are independent, and $(n-1)S_n^2/\sigma^2$ is chi-squared with $n - 1$ degrees of freedom. The proof rotates the data, and the picture is a round cloud of points.
\item Student's $t$ distribution, $Z/\sqrt{V/k}$: heavier tails than the Gaussian, converging to it as $k$ grows, and the Cauchy distribution of \cref{ch:mom-mestimation} when $k = 1$.
\item The $t$-test and the $t$-interval, for one sample and for two (pooled when the variances agree, Welch's approximation when they do not), with exact level for Gaussian data and asymptotic level for any data.
\item Many tests at once: the family-wise error rate and Bonferroni's correction, the false discovery rate and the Benjamini--Hochberg procedure.
\end{itemize}
\Cref{ch:tests-foundations} built one test from the Central Limit Theorem and \cref{ch:mom-mestimation} closed the estimation recipes. This chapter removes the limit theorems from the test and asks what is exactly true at every $n$.
\end{chaptergoals}

\section{When the sample is small}
\label{sec:t-small}

A home is a candidate for a grid-connected wind turbine if its mean wind speed is at least $10$ miles per hour.
Wind was measured once a month for a year: $n = 12$ observations, mean $14.3$, standard deviation $4.7$.
Is the mean wind speed above $10$?
The test of \cref{ch:tests-foundations} would compute $T_n = \sqrt{12}\,(14.3 - 10)/4.7 = 3.17$, compare it with $q_{0.05} = 1.645$, and reject.
That test's guarantee was an \textit{asymptotic} level: by the CLT the standardized mean is approximately Gaussian, and by Slutsky's theorem replacing $\sigma$ by an estimate does not change the limit.
Both are statements about $n \to \infty$, and here $n = 12$.
With twelve numbers there is no limit to appeal to.

What is exactly true at $n = 12$?
Suppose the twelve measurements are modeled as $\Xton \iid \Gauss(\mu, \sigma^2)$.
Then by \cref{ch:gaussian}, $\bar{X}_n \sim \Gauss(\mu, \sigma^2/n)$ exactly, so
\[
\sqrt{n}\,\frac{\bar{X}_n - \mu}{\sigma} \sim \Gauss(0, 1) \qquad \text{for every } n,
\]
with no CLT.
If $\sigma$ were known, the test of \cref{ch:tests-foundations} would have exact level $\alpha$ at $n = 12$, or at $n = 2$.
It is not known, and replacing it by the sample standard deviation
\[
S_n = \sqrt{S_n^2}, \qquad S_n^2 = \frac{1}{n-1}\sum_{i=1}^n (X_i - \bar{X}_n)^2,
\]
is where the exactness is lost: $\sqrt{n}(\bar{X}_n - \mu)/S_n$ is not Gaussian.
\Cref{fig:tpivotsim} shows what it is instead.
On the left, $50{,}000$ samples of $n = 5$ Gaussian observations were drawn, and for each the ratio $T = \sqrt{n}(\bar{X}_n - \mu)/S_n$ was computed.
The histogram is symmetric and bell-shaped, but it is not the standard Gaussian drawn over it in black: the peak is lower and the tails are fatter.
A ratio whose denominator is itself random, and sometimes small, takes large values more often than a ratio with a fixed denominator.
The dashed orange curve, which fits the histogram, is the distribution this chapter derives.
On the right, the price of ignoring the difference: the Gaussian rule `reject when $\abs{T} > 1.96$', meant to reject a true null $5\%$ of the time, rejects it $12\%$ of the time at $n = 5$ and $8\%$ at $n = 10$, and is still $6\%$ at $n = 30$.
Of $1{,}000$ studies with five Gaussian observations each and a true null, the Gaussian rule rejects about $121$, not $50$.

\begin{intuition}[dividing by an estimate adds noise]
The numerator $\sqrt{n}(\bar{X}_n - \mu)$ is Gaussian with standard deviation $\sigma$.
Dividing by the constant $\sigma$ standardizes it.
Dividing by $S_n$ divides by a random variable that is near $\sigma$ on average but wanders, and when the sample happens to be unusually tight, $S_n$ is small and the ratio is inflated.
At $n = 5$, $S_n$ is below $0.6\sigma$ in about one sample in six, and each such sample stretches its $T$ by a factor above $1.6$.
That is where the fat tails come from, and it is why the effect fades as $n$ grows: $S_n \convp \sigma$ by the Law of Large Numbers, so for large $n$ dividing by $S_n$ is almost dividing by $\sigma$.
\end{intuition}

The plan of the chapter is to find the exact distribution of $\sqrt{n}(\bar{X}_n - \mu)/S_n$.
Write it as
\begin{equation}
\sqrt{n}\,\frac{\bar{X}_n - \mu}{S_n} = \frac{\sqrt{n}(\bar{X}_n - \mu)/\sigma}{\sqrt{S_n^2/\sigma^2}} = \frac{Z}{\sqrt{S_n^2/\sigma^2}}, \qquad Z \sim \Gauss(0, 1).
\label{eq:t-decomposition}
\end{equation}
Dividing numerator and denominator by $\sigma$ changes nothing, and it makes both pieces free of $\mu$ and $\sigma$: the numerator is a standard Gaussian, and the denominator is $S_n^2/\sigma^2$, whose distribution does not depend on $\mu$ or $\sigma$ either, since shifting the data leaves $S_n^2$ unchanged and scaling them by a constant scales $S_n^2$ by its square.
Two questions remain: what is the distribution of $S_n^2/\sigma^2$, and how does it relate to the numerator?
The chi-squared distribution answers the first (\cref{sec:t-chisq}), Cochran's theorem the second (\cref{sec:t-cochran}), and Student's $t$ distribution is the name of the ratio (\cref{sec:t-student}).
Because the ratio's distribution depends on neither $\mu$ nor $\sigma$, it is a pivot in the sense of \cref{ch:confidence-intervals}, and the test and interval built on it (\cref{sec:t-test}) are exact at every $n$.

\section{The chi-squared distribution}
\label{sec:t-chisq}

$S_n^2/\sigma^2$ is, up to the factor $1/(n-1)$, a sum of squared deviations divided by $\sigma^2$: a sum of squares of things that are nearly standard Gaussians.
So the distribution of a sum of squared standard Gaussians is the one to study.

\begin{definition}[Chi-squared distribution]
\label{def:chisq}
For a positive integer $k$, the \textbf{chi-squared distribution with $k$ degrees of freedom}, written $\chi^2_k$, is the distribution of
\[
V = Z_1^2 + Z_2^2 + \cdots + Z_k^2, \qquad Z_1, \ldots, Z_k \iid \Gauss(0, 1).
\]
Its quantile with probability $\alpha$ above it is written $q_\alpha(\chi^2_k)$.
\end{definition}

\begin{inwords}
\textbf{In words.} Take $k$ independent standard Gaussians, square each, add. The result is positive, since squares are, and it is the squared length of the vector $(Z_1, \ldots, Z_k)$: the squared distance from the origin of a random point whose coordinates are independent standard Gaussians. `Degrees of freedom' counts the independent Gaussians that went in.
\par\smallskip
\textbf{For the kiss data.} \Cref{ch:tests-foundations} tested $p = \frac12$ with the standardized statistic $T_n = \sqrt{124}\,(0.645 - 0.5)/0.5 = 3.23$, approximately $\Gauss(0,1)$ under the null, and rejected two-sidedly when $\abs{T_n} > 1.96$. The same test can be written with one squared Gaussian: $T_n^2 = 10.45$ is approximately $\chi^2_1$ under the null, and $\abs{T_n} > 1.96$ is the same event as $T_n^2 > 1.96^2 = 3.84 = q_{0.05}(\chi^2_1)$. The two-sided $p$-value $\Pr(\abs{Z} > 3.23) = \Pr(\chi^2_1 > 10.45) = 0.0012$ comes out the same either way. Squaring turns a two-sided Gaussian test into a one-sided chi-squared test, which is how \cref{ch:wald-lrt} tests several parameters at once.
\end{inwords}

\begin{proposition}[Properties of the chi-squared distribution]
\label{prop:chisq-props}
Let $V \sim \chi^2_k$.
\begin{enumerate}[nosep]
\item $\E[V] = k$ and $\var(V) = 2k$.
\item If $V \sim \chi^2_k$ and $W \sim \chi^2_l$ are independent, then $V + W \sim \chi^2_{k+l}$.
\item $V$ has density $f_k(v) = \dfrac{1}{2^{k/2}\,\Gamma(k/2)}\, v^{k/2 - 1} e^{-v/2}$ for $v > 0$, where $\Gamma(x) = \int_0^\infty s^{x-1}e^{-s}\,\mathrm{d}s$ is the Gamma function, which satisfies $\Gamma(x + 1) = x\Gamma(x)$, $\Gamma(n) = (n-1)!$ for integers and $\Gamma(\frac12) = \sqrt{\pi}$. For $k = 1$, $f_1(v) = e^{-v/2}/\sqrt{2\pi v}$; for $k = 2$, $f_2(v) = \frac12 e^{-v/2}$, the $\Expo(\frac12)$ density.
\item As $k \to \infty$, $(V - k)/\sqrt{2k} \convd \Gauss(0, 1)$.
\end{enumerate}
\end{proposition}

\recall{The Central Limit Theorem (\cref{thm:clt}): for i.i.d.\ $Y_i$ with mean $m$ and variance $s^2 < \infty$, $\sqrt{k}(\bar{Y}_k - m) \convd \Gauss(0, s^2)$.}

\optionalproof{Everything follows from the definition as a sum of independent squares: means and variances add, two sums of squares concatenate, and a sum of $k$ i.i.d.\ terms obeys the CLT. The density for $k = 1$ is a change of variables; the general density is a convolution, done in \cref{sec:proof-chisq-density}.}
\begin{proof}
(1) $\E[Z^2] = \var(Z) = 1$, so $\E[V] = k$.
For the variance, $\var(Z^2) = \E[Z^4] - (\E[Z^2])^2 = \E[Z^4] - 1$, and integrating by parts with $u = z^3$ and $\mathrm{d}v = z\varphi(z)\,\mathrm{d}z = -\mathrm{d}\varphi(z)$, where $\varphi$ is the standard Gaussian density,
\[
\E[Z^4] = \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} z^3 \cdot z\varphi(z)\,\mathrm{d}z = \Big[-z^3\varphi(z)\Big]_{-\infty}^{\infty} + 3\int_{-\infty}^{\infty} z^2\varphi(z)\,\mathrm{d}z = 0 + 3\E[Z^2] = 3.
\]
So $\var(Z^2) = 2$, and the variance of a sum of $k$ independent terms is $2k$.

(2) The distribution of $V + W$ depends only on the joint distribution of $(V, W)$, which under independence is the same however the two are constructed, so take $V$ to be a sum of $k$ squared independent standard Gaussians and $W$ a sum of $l$ others, independent of the first $k$. Then $V + W$ is a sum of $k + l$ squared i.i.d.\ standard Gaussians.

(3) For $k = 1$ and $v > 0$, $\Pr(Z^2 \leq v) = \Pr(-\sqrt{v} \leq Z \leq \sqrt{v}) = 2\Phi(\sqrt{v}) - 1$, and differentiating in $v$ \toolkit{sec:math-density} gives the density $2\varphi(\sqrt{v})/(2\sqrt{v}) = e^{-v/2}/\sqrt{2\pi v}$, which is the formula with $\Gamma(\frac12) = \sqrt{\pi}$.
The general formula follows by adding one squared Gaussian at a time, a convolution computed in \cref{sec:proof-chisq-density}; nothing later depends on the formula itself, only on the definition and on (1), (2) and (4).

(4) $V = \sum_{i=1}^k Z_i^2$ is a sum of $k$ i.i.d.\ terms with mean $1$ and variance $2$, so by the CLT, $\sqrt{k}(V/k - 1) \convd \Gauss(0, 2)$, which is the claim after dividing by $\sqrt{2}$.
\end{proof}

\Cref{fig:chisqdens} draws the densities.
For $k = 1$ the density is infinite at $0$ (a standard Gaussian is near $0$ often, so its square is near $0$ very often) and falls away; for $k = 2$ it is the exponential; from $k = 3$ on it starts at $0$, rises to a peak and has a long right tail.
The peak sits at $k - 2$ and the mean at $k$, so the distribution is skewed to the right, with the mean pulled past the peak by the tail.
As $k$ grows the shape becomes symmetric: on the right of the figure, $\chi^2_{30}$ is close to $\Gauss(30, 60)$, the Gaussian with its mean and variance, as statement (4) says it should be, with a remaining lean to the right.

\begin{sanitycheck}[Where should the peak be?]
A $\chi^2_k$ variable has mean $k$ and standard deviation $\sqrt{2k}$: for $k = 10$, mean $10$ and standard deviation $4.5$; for $k = 100$, mean $100$ and standard deviation $14$.
The spread grows like $\sqrt{k}$ while the mean grows like $k$, so relative to its mean the distribution tightens: $V/k$ has standard deviation $\sqrt{2/k}$, which is the Law of Large Numbers for the average of $k$ squared Gaussians.
That is why the sample variance, an average of squared deviations, is a reliable estimate of $\sigma^2$ for large $n$ and an unreliable one for small $n$: at $n = 5$ its relative standard deviation is $\sqrt{2/4} = 0.71$, so a sample variance half or double the truth is routine.
\end{sanitycheck}

\begin{worked}[two sums of squares]
\textit{A known mean.} If $\Xton \iid \Gauss(0, \sigma^2)$, then $X_i/\sigma \iid \Gauss(0,1)$ and
\[
\frac{1}{\sigma^2}\sum_{i=1}^n X_i^2 = \sum_{i=1}^n \Big(\frac{X_i}{\sigma}\Big)^2 \sim \chi^2_n.
\]
So the sample second moment $V_n = \frac1n\sum_i X_i^2$ satisfies $nV_n/\sigma^2 \sim \chi^2_n$, with mean $n$: $\E[V_n] = \sigma^2$, and $\var(V_n) = 2\sigma^4/n$.
For $n = 12$ measurements with a known mean of zero, the variance estimate has relative standard deviation $\sqrt{2/12} = 0.41$.

\textit{Darts.} A dart lands at $(Z_1, Z_2)$ with independent standard Gaussian coordinates.
The probability of landing inside the unit disk is $\Pr(Z_1^2 + Z_2^2 \leq 1) = \Pr(\chi^2_2 \leq 1) = 1 - e^{-1/2} = 0.393$, since $\chi^2_2$ is $\Expo(\frac12)$: about $39$ of every $100$ throws.
\end{worked}

\section{Cochran's theorem}
\label{sec:t-cochran}

Now the denominator of \eqref{eq:t-decomposition}.
Writing $Z_i = (X_i - \mu)/\sigma$, which are i.i.d.\ $\Gauss(0,1)$,
\[
\frac{(n-1)S_n^2}{\sigma^2} = \sum_{i=1}^n \Big(\frac{X_i - \bar{X}_n}{\sigma}\Big)^2 = \sum_{i=1}^n (Z_i - \bar{Z}_n)^2.
\]
If $\bar{Z}_n$ were replaced by the true mean $0$, this would be $\sum_i Z_i^2 \sim \chi^2_n$ by the worked example.
It is not: the deviations are measured from the sample mean, which was itself computed from the same $Z_i$, and the $n$ terms $Z_i - \bar{Z}_n$ are neither independent nor standard Gaussian (they sum to zero, so any $n - 1$ of them determine the last).
The answer is that one degree of freedom is spent on the mean: the sum is $\chi^2_{n-1}$, and it is independent of $\bar{Z}_n$.

\begin{intuition}[a Gaussian cloud is round, so rotate it]
Draw $n = 2$ independent standard Gaussians as a point $(Z_1, Z_2)$ in the plane and repeat $1{,}500$ times (\cref{fig:gaussrotation}, left).
The cloud is round: its density $\frac{1}{2\pi}e^{-(z_1^2 + z_2^2)/2}$ depends only on the distance from the origin, so the cloud looks the same from every direction.
Now describe each point not by its coordinates along the usual axes but along two rotated axes, $u_1$ in the direction $(1, 1)/\sqrt{2}$ and $u_2$ in the direction $(1, -1)/\sqrt{2}$.
The coordinate along $u_1$ is $Y_1 = (Z_1 + Z_2)/\sqrt{2} = \sqrt{2}\,\bar{Z}_2$: it carries the sample mean.
The coordinate along $u_2$ is $Y_2 = (Z_1 - Z_2)/\sqrt{2}$, and $Y_2^2 = (Z_1 - Z_2)^2/2 = (Z_1 - \bar{Z}_2)^2 + (Z_2 - \bar{Z}_2)^2$: it carries the sum of squared deviations.
Because the cloud is round, the picture in the rotated coordinates (right) is the same round cloud: $Y_1$ and $Y_2$ are again independent standard Gaussians.
So the sample mean and the sum of squared deviations are built from two independent standard Gaussians, one each, and the sum of squares is $Y_2^2 \sim \chi^2_1$: one degree of freedom, not two because the other one went to the mean.
For general $n$ the same rotation works in $n$ dimensions: one axis for the mean, $n - 1$ for the deviations.
\end{intuition}

\end{figure}

The fact that makes the picture a proof is in the Toolkit \toolkit{sec:math-gaussvec}: if $Z = (Z_1, \ldots, Z_n)$ has i.i.d.\ $\Gauss(0,1)$ coordinates and $Q$ is an orthogonal matrix (a rotation, possibly with reflections: $Q^\top Q = I$), then $Y = QZ$ again has i.i.d.\ $\Gauss(0,1)$ coordinates, and $\norm{Y} = \norm{Z}$.

\begin{theorem}[Cochran's theorem for the sample mean and variance]
\label{thm:cochran}
Let $\Xton \iid \Gauss(\mu, \sigma^2)$ with $n \geq 2$.
Then
\begin{enumerate}[nosep]
\item $\bar{X}_n$ and $S_n^2$ are independent;
\item $\dfrac{(n-1)S_n^2}{\sigma^2} \sim \chi^2_{n-1}$.
\end{enumerate}
\end{theorem}

\coreproof{Standardize to $Z_i = (X_i - \mu)/\sigma$ and rotate: pick an orthogonal $Q$ whose first row is $(1, \ldots, 1)/\sqrt{n}$. The first rotated coordinate is $\sqrt{n}\bar{Z}_n$; the sum of squared deviations is the squared length of the other $n - 1$ coordinates, since rotation preserves length. The rotated coordinates are i.i.d.\ standard Gaussians, so the two pieces are independent and the second is a sum of $n - 1$ squares.}
\begin{proof}
Let $Z_i = (X_i - \mu)/\sigma$, so $Z = (Z_1, \ldots, Z_n)$ has i.i.d.\ $\Gauss(0,1)$ coordinates, $\bar{X}_n = \mu + \sigma\bar{Z}_n$ and $(n-1)S_n^2/\sigma^2 = \sum_i (Z_i - \bar{Z}_n)^2$.
It is enough to prove the two statements for $\bar{Z}_n$ and $\sum_i (Z_i - \bar{Z}_n)^2$.

Let $Q$ be an $n \times n$ orthogonal matrix whose first row is $(1/\sqrt{n}, \ldots, 1/\sqrt{n})$; one exists because any unit vector can be extended to an orthonormal basis \toolkit{sec:math-gaussvec}.
Set $Y = QZ$.
By the Toolkit, $Y_1, \ldots, Y_n$ are i.i.d.\ $\Gauss(0,1)$, and $\norm{Y}^2 = \norm{Z}^2$.
The first coordinate is
\[
Y_1 = \frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\sum_{i=1}^n Z_i = \sqrt{n}\,\bar{Z}_n.
\]
Expanding the squared deviations,
\[
\sum_{i=1}^n (Z_i - \bar{Z}_n)^2 = \sum_{i=1}^n Z_i^2 - n\bar{Z}_n^2 = \norm{Z}^2 - Y_1^2 = \norm{Y}^2 - Y_1^2 = Y_2^2 + \cdots + Y_n^2,
\]
where the first equality expands the square (the cross term is $-2\bar{Z}_n\sum_i Z_i = -2n\bar{Z}_n^2$) and the third is $\norm{QZ} = \norm{Z}$.
So $\bar{Z}_n = Y_1/\sqrt{n}$ is a function of $Y_1$ alone and $\sum_i(Z_i - \bar{Z}_n)^2$ a function of $(Y_2, \ldots, Y_n)$ alone; since the joint density of $Y$ factors into a function of $y_1$ times a function of $(y_2, \ldots, y_n)$ \toolkit{sec:math-gaussvec}, any function of the first is independent of any function of the rest, which is (1).
And $Y_2^2 + \cdots + Y_n^2$ is a sum of $n - 1$ squared i.i.d.\ standard Gaussians, which is (2).
\end{proof}

\begin{corollary}[Mean and variance of the sample variance]
\label{cor:samplevar}
For $\Xton \iid \Gauss(\mu, \sigma^2)$, $\E[S_n^2] = \sigma^2$ and $\var(S_n^2) = \dfrac{2\sigma^4}{n-1}$.
\end{corollary}
\optionalproof{$S_n^2$ is a chi-squared variable with $n - 1$ degrees of freedom, scaled by $\sigma^2/(n-1)$; scale its mean and variance.}
\begin{proof}
$S_n^2 = \sigma^2 V/(n-1)$ with $V \sim \chi^2_{n-1}$, so $\E[S_n^2] = \sigma^2(n-1)/(n-1)$ and $\var(S_n^2) = \sigma^4 \cdot 2(n-1)/(n-1)^2$.
\end{proof}

The unbiasedness was proved for any distribution in \cref{ch:estimators}; the variance formula is new, and Gaussian.
For the wind data, $n = 12$: the sample variance has standard deviation $\sigma^2\sqrt{2/11} = 0.43\sigma^2$, so the observed $4.7^2 = 22.1$ is a rough estimate of $\sigma^2$, which is exactly why the chapter cannot pretend $S_n$ is $\sigma$.

\begin{sanitycheck}[Cochran at $n = 2$, by hand]
With two observations, $\bar{X}_2 = (X_1 + X_2)/2$ and $S_2^2 = (X_1 - X_2)^2/2$ (both deviations from the mean equal $\pm(X_1 - X_2)/2$).
The theorem says $S_2^2/\sigma^2 = \big((X_1 - X_2)/(\sqrt{2}\sigma)\big)^2 \sim \chi^2_1$, which checks: $X_1 - X_2 \sim \Gauss(0, 2\sigma^2)$ by the sum rule of \cref{ch:gaussian}, so $(X_1 - X_2)/(\sqrt{2}\sigma)$ is a standard Gaussian and its square is $\chi^2_1$.
It also says the sum $X_1 + X_2$ and the difference $X_1 - X_2$ are independent, which is the $45^\circ$ rotation of \cref{fig:gaussrotation}.
Independence here is not obvious: both are functions of the same two numbers.
It holds because the cloud is round, and \cref{ex:t-cochran-two} verifies it directly from the joint density.
\end{sanitycheck}

\begin{pitfall}[the independence is a Gaussian specialty]
\Cref{fig:meanvarindep} plots the sample mean against the sample variance for $4{,}000$ samples of $n = 5$.
For Gaussian data (left) the cloud has no structure: knowing that a sample's mean is high says nothing about its variance.
For exponential data with the same mean and variance (right), samples with a high mean have a high variance, with a correlation of $0.70$.
The reason is the shape of the exponential density: a large sample mean usually means the sample contains one of the distribution's occasional large values, which also inflates the variance.
Cochran's theorem is false for non-Gaussian data, and so is everything built on it, including the exactness of the $t$-test.
\Cref{sec:t-test} says what survives.
\end{pitfall}

\begin{bridge}[degrees of freedom, and the $n - 1$ for the third time]
`Degrees of freedom' in an introductory course is a number looked up to find the right row of a table, and $n - 1$ is explained, if at all, as `one degree of freedom is lost estimating the mean'.
Cochran's theorem is that sentence made exact.
The $n$ deviations $X_i - \bar{X}_n$ live in an $(n-1)$-dimensional space, since they sum to zero, and in the rotated coordinates they are exactly $n - 1$ independent Gaussians: $n - 1$ is the count of independent squared Gaussians in the sample variance, and it is the degrees of freedom of its chi-squared distribution.
\Cref{ch:estimators} gave two earlier readings of the same $n - 1$: it removes the bias of the divide-by-$n$ variance, and it is not the choice that minimizes risk (\cref{ex:t-nplus1} finishes that story with the variance formula of \cref{cor:samplevar}).
\end{bridge}

\begin{remark}[The general theorem]
What is called Cochran's theorem in full is a statement about splitting $\norm{Z}^2$ into several sums of squares, one per subspace in an orthogonal decomposition of $\real^n$: each piece is chi-squared with the dimension of its subspace as degrees of freedom, and the pieces are independent.
\Cref{thm:cochran} is the case of two subspaces, the line through $(1, \ldots, 1)$ and everything perpendicular to it.
\Cref{ch:regression-multi} uses the general case, with the subspace spanned by the regressors in place of the line, to get the exact distribution of the residual variance in Gaussian linear regression; the proof is the one above with a different orthogonal basis.
\end{remark}

\section{Student's \texorpdfstring{$t$}{t} distribution}
\label{sec:t-student}

Both pieces of \eqref{eq:t-decomposition} are now known and independent: the numerator is $Z \sim \Gauss(0,1)$ and the denominator is $\sqrt{V/(n-1)}$ with $V \sim \chi^2_{n-1}$.
The ratio has a name.

\begin{definition}[Student's $t$ distribution]
\label{def:student}
For a positive integer $k$, \textbf{Student's $t$ distribution with $k$ degrees of freedom}, written $t_k$, is the distribution of
\[
T = \frac{Z}{\sqrt{V/k}}, \qquad Z \sim \Gauss(0,1),\ V \sim \chi^2_k,\ Z \text{ and } V \text{ independent}.
\]
Its quantile with probability $\alpha$ above it is written $q_\alpha(t_k)$.
\end{definition}

\begin{inwords}
\textbf{In words.} A standard Gaussian divided by an independent estimate of its own scale, where the estimate is the root of an average of $k$ squared standard Gaussians; the average has mean $1$ and wanders less as $k$ grows. The ratio is symmetric about $0$ like $Z$, and wider than $Z$ because the denominator is sometimes small.
\par\smallskip
\textbf{For the kiss data.} The $t$ statistic is $\sqrt{n}(\bar{R}_n - \frac12)/S_n$ with $S_n^2 = \frac{n}{n-1}\hat{p}(1 - \hat{p}) = 0.231$: $T = \sqrt{124}\,(0.645 - 0.5)/0.480 = 3.36$, against the two-sided threshold $q_{0.025}(t_{123}) = 1.979$ in place of $1.960$. The threshold moved by $0.02$ because at $n = 124$ the denominator hardly wanders; the data are $0$s and $1$s, so the exactness of \cref{thm:cochran} does not apply to them, and \cref{sec:t-test} says why the test is sound anyway.
\end{inwords}

\begin{proposition}[Properties of Student's $t$]
\label{prop:student-props}
Let $T \sim t_k$.
\begin{enumerate}[nosep]
\item $T$ is symmetric about $0$: $-T \sim t_k$, so $q_\alpha(t_k) = -q_{1-\alpha}(t_k)$.
\item $T$ has density $g_k(t) = \dfrac{\Gamma\big(\frac{k+1}{2}\big)}{\sqrt{k\pi}\,\Gamma\big(\frac{k}{2}\big)}\Big(1 + \dfrac{t^2}{k}\Big)^{-(k+1)/2}$. For $k = 1$ this is the Cauchy density $1/(\pi(1 + t^2))$ of \cref{ch:mom-mestimation}.
\item $\E[T] = 0$ for $k \geq 2$ and $\var(T) = \dfrac{k}{k-2}$ for $k \geq 3$; for $k = 1$ the mean does not exist, and for $k = 2$ the variance is infinite.
\item As $k \to \infty$, $t_k \convd \Gauss(0,1)$: for every $t$, $\Pr(T_k \leq t) \to \Phi(t)$, and $q_\alpha(t_k) \to q_\alpha$.
\end{enumerate}
\end{proposition}

\recall{The Law of Large Numbers (\cref{thm:wlln}): for i.i.d.\ $Y_i$ with $\var(Y_i) < \infty$, $\bar{Y}_k \convp \E[Y_1]$. The continuous mapping theorem (\cref{thm:cmt}): if $W_k \convp w$ and $g$ is continuous, then $g(W_k) \convp g(w)$. Slutsky's theorem (\cref{thm:slutsky}): if $X_k \convd X$ and $W_k \convp w \neq 0$, then $X_k/W_k \convd X/w$.}

\optionalproof{Symmetry is the symmetry of $Z$. The limit is the Law of Large Numbers for the denominator plus Slutsky: $V/k$ is an average of $k$ squared Gaussians, so it tends to $1$. The density is a calculation done in \cref{sec:proof-chisq-density} and the moments are an exercise; nothing later depends on them.}
\begin{proof}
(1) $-T = (-Z)/\sqrt{V/k}$ and $-Z \sim \Gauss(0,1)$ is independent of $V$, so $-T$ satisfies the definition.
For the quantiles, $\Pr(T > q) = \alpha$ gives $\Pr(-T < -q) = \alpha$, that is, $\Pr(T < -q) = \alpha$, so $-q$ has probability $1 - \alpha$ above it.

(4) Write $V = Z_1^2 + \cdots + Z_k^2$, independent of $Z$.
Then $V/k$ is the average of $k$ i.i.d.\ variables with mean $1$ and variance $2$, so $V/k \convp 1$ by the Law of Large Numbers, and $\sqrt{V/k} \convp 1$ by the continuous mapping theorem.
The numerator $Z$ does not depend on $k$ at all (a constant sequence converges in distribution to itself), so by Slutsky's theorem $Z/\sqrt{V/k} \convd Z/1 = Z$.
Convergence in distribution to a continuous limit is convergence of the CDFs $F_k$ at every point, and it carries the quantiles along. Fix $\epsilon > 0$. Since $\Phi$ is strictly increasing, $\Phi(q_\alpha - \epsilon) < 1 - \alpha < \Phi(q_\alpha + \epsilon)$, and since $F_k(q_\alpha \pm \epsilon) \to \Phi(q_\alpha \pm \epsilon)$, for all large $k$ we have $F_k(q_\alpha - \epsilon) < 1 - \alpha < F_k(q_\alpha + \epsilon)$; the solution $q_\alpha(t_k)$ of $F_k(q) = 1 - \alpha$ then lies in $(q_\alpha - \epsilon, q_\alpha + \epsilon)$.

(2) is computed in \cref{sec:proof-chisq-density} by conditioning on $V$ and integrating. (3) is \cref{ex:t-variance}, from $\E[1/V] = 1/(k-2)$ for $V \sim \chi^2_k$.
\end{proof}

\begin{theorem}[The $t$ pivot]
\label{thm:t-pivot}
If $\Xton \iid \Gauss(\mu, \sigma^2)$ with $n \geq 2$, then
\[
\sqrt{n}\,\frac{\bar{X}_n - \mu}{S_n} \sim t_{n-1} \qquad \text{exactly, for every } n, \mu \text{ and } \sigma^2.
\]
\end{theorem}

\coreproof{Decompose as in \eqref{eq:t-decomposition}; the numerator is a standard Gaussian, the denominator is $\sqrt{V/(n-1)}$ with $V \sim \chi^2_{n-1}$ by Cochran, and the two are independent by Cochran.}
\begin{proof}
By \eqref{eq:t-decomposition},
\[
\sqrt{n}\,\frac{\bar{X}_n - \mu}{S_n} = \frac{Z}{\sqrt{V/(n-1)}}, \qquad Z = \sqrt{n}\,\frac{\bar{X}_n - \mu}{\sigma}, \quad V = \frac{(n-1)S_n^2}{\sigma^2}.
\]
$Z \sim \Gauss(0,1)$ because $\bar{X}_n \sim \Gauss(\mu, \sigma^2/n)$ (\cref{ch:gaussian}).
$V \sim \chi^2_{n-1}$ by \cref{thm:cochran}(2).
$Z$ is a function of $\bar{X}_n$ and $V$ a function of $S_n^2$, which are independent by \cref{thm:cochran}(1).
So the ratio satisfies \cref{def:student} with $k = n - 1$.
\end{proof}

The distribution was found in 1908 by William Sealy Gosset, a chemist at the Guinness brewery in Dublin who needed to compare small batches of barley; the brewery forbade its staff from publishing, so the paper appeared under the pseudonym `Student'.

\begin{bridge}[the $t$ critical value you were taught]
An introductory course says: when $\sigma$ is unknown, use $s$ and look up the $t$ table with $n - 1$ degrees of freedom instead of the $z$ table.
\Cref{thm:t-pivot} is the reason, and it also says what the rule assumes: the $t$ table is exact when the data are Gaussian and only then.
What that course called `the $t$ distribution gets closer to the normal as $n$ grows' is \cref{prop:student-props}(4), and `use $z$ when $n \geq 30$' is a judgment that $2.04$ is close enough to $1.96$.
\end{bridge}

\section{The \texorpdfstring{$t$}{t}-test and the \texorpdfstring{$t$}{t}-interval}
\label{sec:t-test}

With an exact pivot, the test and the interval follow the recipe of \cref{ch:confidence-intervals,ch:tests-foundations} with the Student quantile in place of the Gaussian one.

\begin{definition}[The one-sample $t$-test]
\label{def:ttest}
Let $\Xton \iid \Gauss(\mu, \sigma^2)$ with $\mu$ and $\sigma^2$ unknown, let $\mu_0$ be a fixed number, and let
\[
T_n = \sqrt{n}\,\frac{\bar{X}_n - \mu_0}{S_n}.
\]
\textbf{Student's $t$-test} at level $\alpha$ is
\[
\begin{array}{lll}
H_0: \mu = \mu_0,\ H_1: \mu \neq \mu_0: & \psi_\alpha = \ind\{\abs{T_n} > q_{\alpha/2}(t_{n-1})\}, & p\text{-value } \Pr(\abs{t_{n-1}} > \abs{T_n^{\text{obs}}}); \\[2pt]
H_0: \mu \leq \mu_0,\ H_1: \mu > \mu_0: & \psi_\alpha = \ind\{T_n > q_{\alpha}(t_{n-1})\}, & p\text{-value } \Pr(t_{n-1} > T_n^{\text{obs}}); \\[2pt]
H_0: \mu \geq \mu_0,\ H_1: \mu < \mu_0: & \psi_\alpha = \ind\{T_n < -q_{\alpha}(t_{n-1})\}, & p\text{-value } \Pr(t_{n-1} < T_n^{\text{obs}}).
\end{array}
\]
where $T_n^{\text{obs}}$ is the value of $T_n$ computed from the data.
The \textbf{$t$-interval} is
\[
\mathcal{I} = \big[\bar{X}_n - q_{\alpha/2}(t_{n-1})\,S_n/\sqrt{n},\ \bar{X}_n + q_{\alpha/2}(t_{n-1})\,S_n/\sqrt{n}\big].
\]
\end{definition}

\begin{inwords}
\textbf{In words.} Standardize the sample mean's distance from the null value by its estimated standard error $S_n/\sqrt{n}$, and compare with a Student cutoff instead of a Gaussian one. The cutoff is larger, by a lot for small $n$ and by a little for large $n$, to pay for the noise in $S_n$. The interval is the usual `estimate $\pm$ cutoff $\times$ standard error' with the Student cutoff.
\par\smallskip
\textbf{For the kiss data.} $T = 3.36$ against $q_{0.025}(t_{123}) = 1.979$: reject $p = \frac12$ at $5\%$, with $p$-value $\Pr(\abs{t_{123}} > 3.36) = 0.0010$. The $t$-interval is $0.645 \pm 1.979 \times 0.480/\sqrt{124} = [0.560, 0.730]$, against the plug-in interval $[0.561, 0.729]$ of \cref{ch:confidence-intervals}: at $n = 124$ the two recipes agree to within $0.001$.
\end{inwords}

\begin{proposition}[Exact level, exact coverage, consistency]
\label{prop:ttest-level}
For $\Xton \iid \Gauss(\mu, \sigma^2)$:
\begin{enumerate}[nosep]
\item Each test in \cref{def:ttest} has level exactly $\alpha$ at every $n \geq 2$: the type 1 error is $\alpha$ when $\mu = \mu_0$, and at most $\alpha$ everywhere in a composite null $\mu \leq \mu_0$ or $\mu \geq \mu_0$.
\item The $t$-interval has coverage exactly $1 - \alpha$ at every $n \geq 2$.
\item Under any fixed alternative $\mu \neq \mu_0$, $\Pr_\mu(\psi_\alpha = 1) \to 1$ as $n \to \infty$.
\end{enumerate}
\end{proposition}

\optionalproof{At $\mu = \mu_0$ the statistic is the pivot of \cref{thm:t-pivot}, whose quantile was chosen to leave probability $\alpha$ outside. For a composite null, moving $\mu$ into the null shifts $T_n$ the wrong way for rejecting, so the type 1 error can only fall. The interval is the pivot trapped between its quantiles and solved for $\mu$. Consistency is the pivot staying put while $\sqrt{n}(\mu - \mu_0)/S_n$ runs off to infinity.}
\begin{proof}
(1) Write $T_n = T_n^* + \sqrt{n}(\mu - \mu_0)/S_n$ with $T_n^* = \sqrt{n}(\bar{X}_n - \mu)/S_n \sim t_{n-1}$ by \cref{thm:t-pivot}, whatever $\mu$ is.
When $\mu = \mu_0$, $T_n = T_n^*$ and $\Pr_{\mu_0}(\abs{T_n} > q_{\alpha/2}(t_{n-1})) = \alpha$ by the definition of the quantile and the symmetry of $t_{n-1}$; likewise $\Pr_{\mu_0}(T_n > q_\alpha(t_{n-1})) = \alpha$.
For the composite null $\mu \leq \mu_0$ of the second test, $\sqrt{n}(\mu - \mu_0)/S_n \leq 0$ since $S_n > 0$, so $T_n \leq T_n^*$ and
\[
\Pr_\mu\big(T_n > q_\alpha(t_{n-1})\big) \leq \Pr\big(T_n^* > q_\alpha(t_{n-1})\big) = \alpha,
\]
with equality at $\mu = \mu_0$.
The third test is the mirror image.

(2) $\Pr_{\mu,\sigma^2}(\mu \in \mathcal{I}) = \Pr\big(\abs{\sqrt{n}(\bar{X}_n - \mu)/S_n} \leq q_{\alpha/2}(t_{n-1})\big) = \Pr(\abs{t_{n-1}} \leq q_{\alpha/2}(t_{n-1})) = 1 - \alpha$.

(3) Take the two-sided test and $\mu > \mu_0$; the one-sided cases are identical.
The threshold $q_{\alpha/2}(t_{n-1})$ depends on $n$, but it is at most $c = q_{\alpha/2}(t_1)$, the largest quantile in the family ($12.7$ at $\alpha = 0.05$), so it is enough to show $\Pr_\mu(T_n > c) \to 1$.
Fix $M > 0$.
Since $T_n^* \sim t_{n-1} \convd \Gauss(0,1)$ by \cref{prop:student-props}(4), $\Pr(T_n^* > -M) \to \Phi(M)$.
Since $S_n \convp \sigma$ (the Law of Large Numbers applied to the $X_i$ and to the $X_i^2$, which have finite variance for Gaussian data, then the continuous mapping theorem for the square root, as in \cref{ch:estimators}), $\sqrt{n}(\mu - \mu_0)/S_n \convp +\infty$: $\Pr(\sqrt{n}(\mu - \mu_0)/S_n > c + M) \to 1$.
On the intersection of the two events, $T_n = T_n^* + \sqrt{n}(\mu - \mu_0)/S_n > -M + c + M = c$, so
\[
\Pr_\mu(T_n > c) \geq \Pr(T_n^* > -M) - \Pr\big(\sqrt{n}(\mu - \mu_0)/S_n \leq c + M\big) \to \Phi(M) - 0.
\]
$M$ was arbitrary and $\Phi(M) \to 1$ as $M \to \infty$, so $\Pr_\mu(T_n > c) \to 1$.
\end{proof}

\begin{worked}[the wind turbine, properly]
$n = 12$, $\bar{X}_n = 14.3$, $S_n = 4.7$, $\mu_0 = 10$, $H_0: \mu \leq 10$ against $H_1: \mu > 10$.
The statistic is $T = \sqrt{12}\,(14.3 - 10)/4.7 = 3.17$, the threshold is $q_{0.05}(t_{11}) = 1.80$ (not $1.645$), and the test rejects: the home has enough wind.
The $p$-value is $\Pr(t_{11} > 3.17) = 0.0045$: of $1{,}000$ homes whose true mean wind is exactly $10$, about $4$ would produce a year of measurements this far above it.
The Gaussian rule would have reported $\Pr(Z > 3.17) = 0.0008$, overstating the evidence by a factor of six because it trusts a standard deviation estimated from twelve numbers as if it were exact.
The $95\%$ $t$-interval for the mean wind speed is $14.3 \pm 2.20 \times 4.7/\sqrt{12} = [11.3, 17.3]$ miles per hour; the Gaussian version $14.3 \pm 1.96 \times 4.7/\sqrt{12} = [11.6, 17.0]$ is narrower, and its true coverage at $n = 12$ is $\Pr(\abs{t_{11}} \leq 1.96) = 92.4\%$, not $95\%$.
\end{worked}

\begin{worked}[the cookies, with the $t$ version promised in \cref{ch:tests-foundations}]
\Cref{ex:tests-cookies} had $32$ cookies with a mean of $14.77$ chips and standard deviation $4.37$ against a claim of at least $20$: $T = \sqrt{32}\,(14.77 - 20)/4.37 = -6.77$.
\Cref{ch:tests-foundations} compared it with the Gaussian and got a $p$-value of $6 \times 10^{-12}$.
With $31$ degrees of freedom the $p$-value is $\Pr(t_{31} < -6.77) = 7 \times 10^{-8}$: ten thousand times larger, and still decisive.
This is the typical pattern for $n$ around $30$: the $t$ correction changes $p$-values that are small by orders of magnitude, and changes decisions only when the Gaussian $p$-value was already near the level.
\end{worked}

\begin{pitfall}[$z$ in place of $t$ at small $n$]
Using $1.96$ with five observations is not a rounding error.
\Cref{fig:tdensities} (right) gives the true type 1 error of the Gaussian rule on Gaussian data: $12\%$ at $n = 5$, $8\%$ at $n = 10$.
Of $1{,}000$ five-observation studies with a true null, about $122$ reject at the nominal $5\%$; the same $1{,}000$ studies analyzed with $q_{0.025}(t_4) = 2.78$ reject $50$.
In the other direction, a reported two-sided `$p = 0.03$' from a Gaussian table at $n = 5$ corresponds to $\abs{T} = 2.17$, whose $t_4$ $p$-value is $0.10$.
\end{pitfall}

\begin{pitfall}[which variance goes in the denominator]
\Cref{thm:t-pivot} uses $S_n^2$ with $n - 1$.
If the divide-by-$n$ variance $\hat{\sigma}_n^2 = \frac1n\sum_i (X_i - \bar{X}_n)^2$ of \cref{ch:likelihood} is used instead, the pivot changes: since $S_n^2 = \frac{n}{n-1}\hat{\sigma}_n^2$,
\[
\sqrt{n}\,\frac{\bar{X}_n - \mu}{S_n} = \sqrt{n-1}\,\frac{\bar{X}_n - \mu}{\hat{\sigma}_n} \sim t_{n-1}.
\]
So $\sqrt{n}(\bar{X}_n - \mu)/\hat{\sigma}_n$ is $\sqrt{n/(n-1)}$ times a $t_{n-1}$ variable, and comparing it with $t_{n-1}$ quantiles rejects too often; at $n = 5$ the factor is $1.12$.
\Cref{ex:t-mle-pivot} works through the version with the maximum likelihood estimators.
\end{pitfall}

\recall{The Central Limit Theorem (\cref{thm:clt}): for i.i.d.\ $X_i$ with mean $\mu$ and variance $\sigma^2 < \infty$, $\sqrt{n}(\bar{X}_n - \mu)/\sigma \convd \Gauss(0,1)$. Slutsky's theorem (\cref{thm:slutsky}): if $X_n \convd X$ and $W_n \convp w \neq 0$, then $X_n/W_n \convd X/w$.}

\begin{bridge}[when the $t$-test is safe without Gaussian data]
The exactness in \cref{prop:ttest-level} needs Gaussian data; the kiss data are not Gaussian, nor are most data.
What survives is the asymptotic guarantee of \cref{ch:tests-foundations}: for any parent distribution with finite variance, under $H_0$ write $T_n = \big(\sqrt{n}(\bar{X}_n - \mu_0)/\sigma\big)\big/(S_n/\sigma)$; the numerator converges in distribution to $\Gauss(0,1)$ by the CLT, the denominator converges in probability to $1$ since $S_n \convp \sigma$, so $T_n \convd \Gauss(0,1)$ by Slutsky's theorem, and $q_\alpha(t_{n-1}) \to q_\alpha$ by \cref{prop:student-props}(4). So the $t$-test has asymptotic level $\alpha$ for every parent.
The $t$-test is therefore never worse than the Gaussian test for large $n$ and exactly right for Gaussian data at every $n$, which is why it is the default.
For small $n$ and non-Gaussian data neither guarantee applies.
Mild departures from the Gaussian shape (a little skew, slightly heavy tails) move the true level only slightly because the sample mean is already close to Gaussian at modest $n$; heavy tails and outliers move it a lot, for the reasons of \cref{ch:mom-mestimation}, and a robust center is then the better estimator.
Whether a sample looks Gaussian can itself be tested, which is what the Lilliefors test and the QQ plot of \cref{ch:ks} do.
\end{bridge}

\section{Two samples}
\label{sec:t-two}

The boarding question of \cref{ch:tests-foundations} compared two airlines, and a clinical trial compares a treated group with a control group.
The model: $\Xton \iid \Gauss(\mu_1, \sigma_1^2)$ and $Y_1, \ldots, Y_m \iid \Gauss(\mu_2, \sigma_2^2)$, the two samples independent, and the hypothesis $H_0: \mu_1 = \mu_2$ against a one- or two-sided alternative.
The natural statistic is the standardized difference of means.
By the sum rule of \cref{ch:gaussian}, $\bar{X}_n - \bar{Y}_m \sim \Gauss\big(\mu_1 - \mu_2, \sigma_1^2/n + \sigma_2^2/m\big)$ exactly, so with known variances
\[
\frac{\bar{X}_n - \bar{Y}_m - (\mu_1 - \mu_2)}{\sqrt{\sigma_1^2/n + \sigma_2^2/m}} \sim \Gauss(0, 1).
\]
With estimated variances the denominator becomes $\sqrt{S_1^2/n + S_2^2/m}$, and whether the result is a $t$ variable depends on whether the two variances are equal.

\begin{theorem}[The pooled two-sample $t$ pivot]
\label{thm:pooled-t}
If in addition $\sigma_1^2 = \sigma_2^2 = \sigma^2$, let
\[
S_p^2 = \frac{(n-1)S_1^2 + (m-1)S_2^2}{n + m - 2}
\]
be the \textbf{pooled variance}, the average of the two sample variances weighted by their degrees of freedom. Then
\[
\frac{\bar{X}_n - \bar{Y}_m - (\mu_1 - \mu_2)}{S_p\sqrt{1/n + 1/m}} \sim t_{n+m-2} \qquad \text{exactly}.
\]
\end{theorem}

\optionalproof{Cochran once per sample gives two independent chi-squareds; their sum is chi-squared with the degrees of freedom added; it is independent of both sample means; the ratio is a $t$ by definition.}
\begin{proof}
By \cref{thm:cochran} applied to each sample, $(n-1)S_1^2/\sigma^2 \sim \chi^2_{n-1}$ and $(m-1)S_2^2/\sigma^2 \sim \chi^2_{m-1}$; they are independent because the samples are, so their sum $V = (n+m-2)S_p^2/\sigma^2 \sim \chi^2_{n+m-2}$ by \cref{prop:chisq-props}(2).
The four quantities $\bar{X}_n, S_1^2, \bar{Y}_m, S_2^2$ are mutually independent (the first two are independent by Cochran, the last two likewise, and the pairs are independent of each other), so $V$ is independent of $\bar{X}_n - \bar{Y}_m$.
With $\sigma_1^2 = \sigma_2^2$ the standardized difference is $Z = (\bar{X}_n - \bar{Y}_m - (\mu_1 - \mu_2))/(\sigma\sqrt{1/n + 1/m}) \sim \Gauss(0,1)$, and
\[
\frac{\bar{X}_n - \bar{Y}_m - (\mu_1 - \mu_2)}{S_p\sqrt{1/n + 1/m}} = \frac{Z}{\sqrt{S_p^2/\sigma^2}} = \frac{Z}{\sqrt{V/(n+m-2)}},
\]
which is $t_{n+m-2}$ by \cref{def:student}.
\end{proof}

The tests and intervals follow as in \cref{def:ttest}, with $\mu_1 - \mu_2$ in place of $\mu$ and $t_{n+m-2}$ in place of $t_{n-1}$; for a composite null such as $\mu_1 - \mu_2 \leq 0$ the argument of \cref{prop:ttest-level}(1) applies unchanged, since the shift term it adds to the pivot is then nonpositive.

When the variances differ, the statistic
\[
T = \frac{\bar{X}_n - \bar{Y}_m - (\mu_1 - \mu_2)}{\sqrt{S_1^2/n + S_2^2/m}}
\]
is still a standard Gaussian divided by an independent estimate of its scale, but the estimate is no longer a scaled chi-squared: $S_1^2/n + S_2^2/m$ is a weighted sum of two chi-squareds with different weights and different degrees of freedom, and its distribution depends on the unknown ratio $\sigma_1^2/\sigma_2^2$.
There is no exact pivot.
The standard remedy, \textbf{Welch's $t$-test}, treats $T$ as approximately $t_\nu$ with the degrees of freedom chosen so that a scaled $\chi^2_\nu$ matches the denominator's mean and variance.

\begin{proposition}[The Welch--Satterthwaite degrees of freedom]
\label{prop:welch}
Let $D = S_1^2/n + S_2^2/m$ and $\delta = \sigma_1^2/n + \sigma_2^2/m = \E[D]$.
The scaled chi-squared $\delta\,W/\nu$ with $W \sim \chi^2_\nu$ that has the same mean and variance as $D$ has
\[
\nu = \frac{(\sigma_1^2/n + \sigma_2^2/m)^2}{\dfrac{(\sigma_1^2/n)^2}{n-1} + \dfrac{(\sigma_2^2/m)^2}{m-1}},
\]
and the estimate $\hat{\nu}$ with $S_1^2, S_2^2$ in place of $\sigma_1^2, \sigma_2^2$ satisfies $\min(n-1, m-1) \leq \hat{\nu} \leq n + m - 2$.
Welch's test compares $T$ with the quantiles of $t_{\hat{\nu}}$, rounding $\hat{\nu}$ down.
\end{proposition}

\optionalproof{Match two moments: the mean matches automatically, and equating the variances, $2\delta^2/\nu$ against the sum of the two sample variances' variances from \cref{cor:samplevar}, gives $\nu$. The bounds are a one-line inequality each.}
\begin{proof}
$\E[\delta W/\nu] = \delta = \E[D]$ for every $\nu$.
$\var(\delta W/\nu) = 2\delta^2/\nu$, and by \cref{cor:samplevar} and independence, $\var(D) = \frac{2\sigma_1^4}{n^2(n-1)} + \frac{2\sigma_2^4}{m^2(m-1)}$.
Setting the two equal and solving for $\nu$ gives the formula.
For the bounds, write $a = S_1^2/n$ and $b = S_2^2/m$, so $\hat{\nu} = (a+b)^2/\big(a^2/(n-1) + b^2/(m-1)\big)$.
Since $a^2/(n-1) + b^2/(m-1) \leq (a^2 + b^2)/\min(n-1, m-1) \leq (a+b)^2/\min(n-1,m-1)$, $\hat{\nu} \geq \min(n-1, m-1)$.
By the Cauchy--Schwarz inequality, $(a + b)^2 = \big(\sqrt{n-1}\cdot\frac{a}{\sqrt{n-1}} + \sqrt{m-1}\cdot\frac{b}{\sqrt{m-1}}\big)^2 \leq (n + m - 2)\big(\frac{a^2}{n-1} + \frac{b^2}{m-1}\big)$, so $\hat{\nu} \leq n + m - 2$.
\end{proof}

The bounds say what the approximation does: when one sample dominates the variance of the difference, the degrees of freedom are about that sample's $n - 1$; when the two contribute equally with equal sizes, $\hat{\nu}$ is near $n + m - 2$, the pooled count.
As $m \to \infty$ with $n$ fixed, $b \to 0$ and $\hat{\nu} \to n - 1$: the one-sample case.
Rounding $\hat{\nu}$ down is the conservative direction, since a smaller $\nu$ means a larger quantile.

\begin{worked}[a clinical trial with small groups]
A drug is meant to lower LDL cholesterol.
The treated group, $n = 12$, shows a mean decrease of $156.4$ with $S_1 = 22.5$; the control group, $m = 22$, a mean decrease of $132.7$ with $S_2 = 8.7$.
$H_0: \mu_1 - \mu_2 \leq 0$ against $H_1: \mu_1 - \mu_2 > 0$.
The variances differ by a factor of $(22.5/8.7)^2 = 6.7$, so pooling is not appropriate and Welch's test is the one to use:
\[
T = \frac{156.4 - 132.7}{\sqrt{22.5^2/12 + 8.7^2/22}} = 3.51, \qquad \hat{\nu} = \frac{(22.5^2/12 + 8.7^2/22)^2}{\dfrac{(22.5^2/12)^2}{11} + \dfrac{(8.7^2/22)^2}{21}} = 12.8,
\]
rounded down to $12$, between the bounds $11$ and $32$ and close to the lower one because the treated group's variance dominates.
The threshold at $5\%$ is $q_{0.05}(t_{12}) = 1.78$ and the $p$-value is $\Pr(t_{12} > 3.51) = 0.0022$: strong evidence that the drug lowers LDL more than the control.
Of $1{,}000$ trials of this size in which the drug does nothing beyond the control, about $2$ would produce a $T$ this large.
The Gaussian $p$-value would have been $0.0002$, ten times smaller.
\end{worked}

\begin{worked}[the boarding question, finished]
\Cref{ch:tests-foundations} opened with $n = 72$ R2F flights averaging $24.2$ minutes (standard deviation $2.1$) and $m = 56$ WilMA flights averaging $15.9$ (standard deviation $1.3$), and settled the question with a heuristic buffer.
Welch's test: $T = (24.2 - 15.9)/\sqrt{2.1^2/72 + 1.3^2/56} = 8.3/0.302 = 27.4$ with $\hat{\nu} = 120$, against a threshold of $q_{0.05}(t_{120}) = 1.66$.
The $p$-value is below $10^{-15}$; the pooled version, $T = 25.9$ with $126$ degrees of freedom, says the same.
The buffer heuristic was right, and now it comes with a level: the difference of $8.3$ minutes is $27$ estimated standard errors, and nothing about estimating two variances from $72$ and $56$ flights can change that.
\end{worked}

\begin{remark}[The $F$ distribution]
Whether the two variances are equal can itself be tested.
For independent $V \sim \chi^2_k$ and $W \sim \chi^2_l$, the ratio $(V/k)/(W/l)$ has the \textbf{$F$ distribution with $(k, l)$ degrees of freedom}, $F_{k,l}$, and by \cref{thm:cochran}, $(S_1^2/\sigma_1^2)/(S_2^2/\sigma_2^2) \sim F_{n-1, m-1}$ exactly for two Gaussian samples (\cref{ex:t-fratio}).
Under $H_0: \sigma_1^2 = \sigma_2^2$ the ratio $S_1^2/S_2^2$ is an $F_{n-1,m-1}$ pivot, and large or small values reject.
The test is sensitive to non-Gaussian tails, more so than the $t$-test because fourth moments enter the variance of a sample variance.
The $F$ distribution's main role in this book is in \cref{ch:regression-multi}, where a ratio of two independent sums of squares from the general Cochran theorem tests several regression coefficients at once.
\end{remark}

\section{Many tests at once}
\label{sec:t-multiple}

In 2009 a team of neuroscientists placed a dead Atlantic salmon in an fMRI scanner, showed it a series of photographs of people in social situations, and asked it to judge the emotion in each.
The scanner divides the fish into $N = 8{,}064$ small volumes, and in each one a test at level $\alpha = 0.001$ asked whether the measured signal responded to the photographs.
A small cluster of volumes inside the brain cavity came out `significant'.
The fish was dead; every null hypothesis was true; and a handful of rejections is exactly what the level promises.
A test at level $0.001$ rejects a true null once in $1{,}000$ tries, so $8{,}064$ tests produce about $8$ false rejections, and the probability that at least one of $8{,}064$ independent tests rejects is $1 - 0.999^{8064} = 0.9997$.
(The study's own test used the scanner's time series; the simplest model with the same arithmetic is $n = 15$ photograph responses per volume, i.i.d.\ Gaussian with mean $\mu$, and a $t$-test of $\mu = 0$, which is what the simulation below runs.)
\Cref{fig:multipletesting} (left) repeats the experiment with simulated noise: $8{,}064$ $t$-tests on samples of $15$ standard Gaussians, every null true.
The $p$-values are uniformly spread between $0$ and $1$ (\cref{ex:tests-uniform}), about $161$ per bar of width $0.02$, and the inset shows the $p$-values below $0.02$ at a finer scale: the bar below $0.001$ holds this run's false discoveries, $5$ of them, against the $8$ expected.

\begin{figure}[htbp]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{fig_multiple_testing}
\caption{Left: $p$-values of $8{,}064$ two-sided $t$-tests at $n = 15$; dotted: expected count per bar; inset: $p$-values below $0.02$, those below $0.001$ in orange. Right: sorted $p$-values, five above the scale, with Bonferroni's $\alpha/N$ (aqua, dotted) and Benjamini--Hochberg's $i\alpha/N$ (orange, dashed); filled dots: rejected by the latter.}
\label{fig:multipletesting}
\end{figure}

The level controls each test's error separately; it says nothing about the batch.
Two quantities describe the batch.
Let $N$ tests be run, with $N_0$ of the nulls true, and let $R$ be the number of rejections and $V$ the number of false rejections (rejections of true nulls) among them; $V$ is a new use of the letter, unrelated to the chi-squared variables above.

\begin{definition}[Family-wise error rate and false discovery rate]
\label{def:fwer-fdr}
The \textbf{family-wise error rate} is $\mathrm{FWER} = \Pr(V \geq 1)$, the probability of at least one false rejection.
The \textbf{false discovery rate} is $\mathrm{FDR} = \E\big[V/\max(R, 1)\big]$, the expected fraction of the rejections that are false (taken as $0$ when nothing is rejected).
\end{definition}

\begin{inwords}
\textbf{In words.} The FWER asks for no false alarm at all in the whole batch; controlling it at $5\%$ means that in $95$ of $100$ batches, every rejection is a true discovery. The FDR tolerates false alarms as long as they are a small fraction of what is reported; controlling it at $5\%$ means that, on average, $5\%$ of the findings you announce are wrong. The first is the stricter demand: since $V/\max(R,1) \leq \ind\{V \geq 1\}$, $\mathrm{FDR} \leq \mathrm{FWER}$, and anything that controls the FWER controls the FDR, not conversely.
\par\smallskip
\textbf{For the kiss data.} One test, $N = 1$: if the null is true both rates are the level, $\alpha$, and if it is false both are $0$. The salmon's $8{,}064$ tests at $\alpha = 0.001$ have $\mathrm{FWER} = 0.9997$, and since every null is true, every rejection is false and $\mathrm{FDR} = \Pr(R \geq 1) = 0.9997$ as well.
\end{inwords}

Both procedures below work with the $p$-values $P_1, \ldots, P_N$ of the $N$ tests, and both use one fact from \cref{ch:tests-foundations}: under a true simple null with a continuous test statistic, the $p$-value is uniform on $[0, 1]$, so $\Pr(P_i \leq u) = u$ (\cref{ex:tests-uniform}); under a composite null the monotonicity argument of \cref{prop:ttest-level}(1) gives $\Pr(P_i \leq u) \leq u$, which is all Bonferroni needs.

\begin{proposition}[Bonferroni's correction]
\label{prop:bonferroni}
Suppose $\Pr(P_i \leq u) \leq u$ for every true null $i$ and every $u \in [0,1]$, and reject test $i$ when $P_i \leq \alpha/N$.
Then $\mathrm{FWER} \leq \alpha$, whatever the dependence between the tests.
\end{proposition}

\coreproof{The probability of a union is at most the sum of the probabilities, and each true null is rejected with probability $\alpha/N$.}
\begin{proof}
The event $\{V \geq 1\}$ is the union over the true nulls $i$ of $\{P_i \leq \alpha/N\}$.
The indicator of a union is at most the sum of the indicators, since if the union occurs at least one indicator equals $1$ \toolkit{sec:math-indicator}, so taking expectations,
\[
\mathrm{FWER} = \Pr\Big(\bigcup_{i \text{ true null}}\{P_i \leq \alpha/N\}\Big) \leq \sum_{i \text{ true null}} \Pr(P_i \leq \alpha/N) \leq N_0 \cdot \frac{\alpha}{N} \leq \alpha.
\]
No independence was used.
\end{proof}

For the salmon, Bonferroni's level per test is $0.001/8064 = 1.2 \times 10^{-7}$, and the two-sided $t_{14}$ threshold rises from $4.14$ to $9.77$: no volume survives, which is the right answer for a dead fish.
The cost is that for a live subject with a few truly active volumes, a threshold of $9.77$ estimated standard errors will miss most of them.
Bonferroni buys `no false alarm' at the price of power, and the price grows with $N$.

The Benjamini--Hochberg procedure gives up on `no false alarm' and controls the FDR instead.
Sort the $p$-values, $P_{(1)} \leq P_{(2)} \leq \cdots \leq P_{(N)}$, and compare the $i$th smallest with the rising line $i\alpha/N$ rather than with the flat $\alpha/N$.

\begin{theorem}[Benjamini--Hochberg]
\label{thm:bh}
Let $i_{\max} = \max\{i : P_{(i)} \leq i\alpha/N\}$ (and $0$ if no $i$ qualifies), and reject the $i_{\max}$ tests with the smallest $p$-values.
If the $p$-values of the true nulls are uniform on $[0,1]$ and independent of each other and of the other $p$-values, then
\[
\mathrm{FDR} = \frac{N_0}{N}\,\alpha \leq \alpha.
\]
\end{theorem}

The proof is in \cref{sec:proof-bh}; nothing later in the book depends on it.
The picture on the right of \cref{fig:multipletesting} is the procedure: ten sorted $p$-values, $0.001, 0.008, 0.012, 0.03, 0.06, \ldots$, against the Bonferroni line at $0.005$ and the Benjamini--Hochberg line $0.005\,i$.
Bonferroni rejects only the first.
Benjamini--Hochberg finds the last point under its line, the third ($0.012 \leq 0.015$; the fourth, $0.03$, is above $0.02$), and rejects the first three, including the second, whose $p$-value $0.008$ is above $0.005$ on its own.
The rising line is the point: a batch with several small $p$-values is evidence that some nulls are false, and the procedure lets the small ones vouch for each other.
The theorem's $N_0/N$ says the control is conservative when many nulls are false; when every null is true, $V = R$, so $\mathrm{FDR} = \mathrm{FWER}$ and the procedure holds the probability of any false rejection at exactly $\alpha$, where Bonferroni holds it at most $\alpha$.

\begin{pitfall}[FWER and FDR are different promises]
`At most $5\%$ of my $100$ discoveries are false on average' and `with probability $95\%$ none of my discoveries is false' are regularly conflated.
A procedure with $\mathrm{FDR} \leq 0.05$ that reports $100$ discoveries is expected to contain about $5$ false ones; a procedure with $\mathrm{FWER} \leq 0.05$ contains none in $19$ batches of $20$.
Which one is wanted depends on what a false discovery costs: a follow-up experiment, or a published claim.
\end{pitfall}

\section{Summary}
\label{sec:t-summary}

\begin{itemize}[nosep]
\item For Gaussian data, $\sqrt{n}(\bar{X}_n - \mu)/\sigma$ is exactly standard Gaussian at every $n$; replacing $\sigma$ by $S_n$ breaks this, and the Gaussian $5\%$ rule has type 1 error $12\%$ at $n = 5$.
\item $\chi^2_k$ is the sum of $k$ squared independent standard Gaussians: mean $k$, variance $2k$, additive, approximately $\Gauss(k, 2k)$ for large $k$; $\chi^2_2$ is $\Expo(\frac12)$.
\item Cochran: for Gaussian data $\bar{X}_n$ and $S_n^2$ are independent and $(n-1)S_n^2/\sigma^2 \sim \chi^2_{n-1}$, proved by rotating the standardized data so that one coordinate carries the mean and $n - 1$ carry the deviations. $\var(S_n^2) = 2\sigma^4/(n-1)$. The independence fails for non-Gaussian data.
\item $t_k$ is $Z/\sqrt{V/k}$ with $Z$ and $V \sim \chi^2_k$ independent: symmetric, heavier tails than the Gaussian (variance $k/(k-2)$), converging to it as $k \to \infty$ by the LLN and Slutsky; $t_1$ is the Cauchy.
\item The $t$ pivot: $\sqrt{n}(\bar{X}_n - \mu)/S_n \sim t_{n-1}$ exactly for Gaussian data. The $t$-test and $t$-interval have exact level and coverage at every $n$ for Gaussian data, and asymptotic level for any parent with finite variance.
\item Two samples: with equal variances the pooled statistic is exactly $t_{n+m-2}$; with unequal variances Welch's statistic is approximately $t_{\hat{\nu}}$, with $\hat{\nu}$ from matching the denominator's mean and variance, between $\min(n,m) - 1$ and $n + m - 2$.
\item Many tests: Bonferroni's $\alpha/N$ controls the probability of any false rejection, with no assumption on dependence; Benjamini--Hochberg's rising threshold $i\alpha/N$ controls the expected fraction of false rejections, for independent $p$-values, and rejects more.
\end{itemize}

\section{Exercises}
\label{sec:t-exercises}

\begin{exercise}
\label{ex:t-chisq-moments}
Let $\Xton \iid \Gauss(3, \sigma^2)$ with $\sigma^2$ unknown and the mean known to be $3$. (a) Find a constant $a$ and an integer $k$ such that $a\sum_i (X_i - 3)^2 \sim \chi^2_k$. (b) Compute the mean and variance of the estimator $\tilde{\sigma}^2 = \frac1n\sum_i(X_i - 3)^2$ and compare its variance with that of $S_n^2$. (c) A dart lands at $(Z_1, Z_2)$ with i.i.d.\ standard Gaussian coordinates. What is the probability that it lands within distance $2$ of the origin?
\end{exercise}
\begin{solution}
(a) $(X_i - 3)/\sigma \iid \Gauss(0,1)$, so $a = 1/\sigma^2$ and $k = n$. (b) $\tilde{\sigma}^2 = \sigma^2 V/n$ with $V \sim \chi^2_n$: mean $\sigma^2$, variance $2\sigma^4/n$, against $2\sigma^4/(n-1)$ for $S_n^2$. Knowing the mean buys one degree of freedom. (c) $\Pr(Z_1^2 + Z_2^2 \leq 4) = \Pr(\chi^2_2 \leq 4) = 1 - e^{-2} = 0.865$.
\end{solution}

\begin{exercise}
\label{ex:t-cochran-two}
Let $X_1, X_2 \iid \Gauss(0, 1)$ and set $U = (X_1 + X_2)/\sqrt{2}$, $W = (X_1 - X_2)/\sqrt{2}$. Starting from the joint density of $(X_1, X_2)$, show that $(U, W)$ has the same joint density, and conclude that $U$ and $W$ are independent standard Gaussians. Deduce \cref{thm:cochran} for $n = 2$.
\end{exercise}
\begin{solution}
The joint density of $(X_1, X_2)$ is $\frac{1}{2\pi}e^{-(x_1^2 + x_2^2)/2}$. The map $(x_1, x_2) \mapsto (u, w) = ((x_1 + x_2)/\sqrt{2}, (x_1 - x_2)/\sqrt{2})$ is linear with matrix $Q = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\begin{pmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 1 & -1\end{pmatrix}$, which is orthogonal ($Q^\top Q = I$, $\det Q = -1$), and $u^2 + w^2 = x_1^2 + x_2^2$. By the change of variables formula \toolkit{sec:math-gaussvec}, the density of $(U, W)$ is $\frac{1}{2\pi}e^{-(u^2 + w^2)/2}/\abs{\det Q} = \frac{1}{2\pi}e^{-u^2/2}e^{-w^2/2}$, a product of two standard Gaussian densities, so $U$ and $W$ are independent standard Gaussians. Then $\bar{X}_2 = U/\sqrt{2}$ and $S_2^2 = (X_1 - X_2)^2/2 = W^2 \sim \chi^2_1$, independent of $\bar{X}_2$.
\end{solution}

\begin{exercise}
\label{ex:t-nplus1}
For $\Xton \iid \Gauss(\mu, \sigma^2)$ consider the estimators $\hat{\sigma}^2_c = c\sum_i(X_i - \bar{X}_n)^2$ of $\sigma^2$. Using \cref{thm:cochran}, find the bias, variance and quadratic risk of $\hat{\sigma}^2_c$ as functions of $c$, and show that the risk is minimized at $c = 1/(n+1)$, not at $1/(n-1)$ (unbiased) or $1/n$ (maximum likelihood). Compute the three risks for $n = 12$ and $\sigma = 1$.
\end{exercise}
\begin{solution}
$\sum_i(X_i - \bar{X}_n)^2 = \sigma^2 V$ with $V \sim \chi^2_{n-1}$, so $\E[\hat{\sigma}^2_c] = c(n-1)\sigma^2$, bias $(c(n-1) - 1)\sigma^2$, variance $2c^2(n-1)\sigma^4$, and risk $\sigma^4\big[2c^2(n-1) + (c(n-1) - 1)^2\big]$. The derivative in $c$ is $\sigma^4[4c(n-1) + 2(n-1)(c(n-1) - 1)]$, zero at $c(2 + n - 1) = 1$, that is, $c = 1/(n+1)$, a minimum since the risk is a convex parabola in $c$. The risks are $2\sigma^4/(n-1)$ for $c = 1/(n-1)$, $(2n-1)\sigma^4/n^2$ for $c = 1/n$ and $2\sigma^4/(n+1)$ for $c = 1/(n+1)$: at $n = 12$, $0.182$, $0.160$ and $0.154$. This is the claim of the bridge in \cref{ch:estimators}.
\end{solution}

\begin{exercise}
\label{ex:t-variance}
Let $V \sim \chi^2_k$ with density $f_k$ from \cref{prop:chisq-props}. (a) Show that $\E[1/V] = 1/(k-2)$ for $k \geq 3$, using $\Gamma(x + 1) = x\Gamma(x)$. (b) Deduce that $\var(T) = k/(k-2)$ for $T \sim t_k$, $k \geq 3$, and explain why $\E[T] = 0$ needs $k \geq 2$. (c) What are the variances of $t_3$, $t_5$ and $t_{30}$, and what do they say about the table of quantiles in \cref{sec:t-student}?
\end{exercise}
\begin{solution}
(a) $\E[1/V] = \frac{1}{2^{k/2}\Gamma(k/2)}\int_0^\infty v^{k/2 - 2}e^{-v/2}\,\mathrm{d}v$. Substituting $v = 2s$, the integral is $2^{k/2 - 1}\int_0^\infty s^{k/2 - 2}e^{-s}\,\mathrm{d}s$, which is $2^{k/2-1}\Gamma(k/2 - 1)$, finite when $k/2 - 1 > 0$. So $\E[1/V] = \frac12\Gamma(k/2 - 1)/\Gamma(k/2) = \frac12 \cdot \frac{1}{k/2 - 1} = 1/(k-2)$. (b) By independence, $\E[T^2] = \E[Z^2]\,\E[k/V] = k/(k-2)$, and $\E[T] = \E[Z]\,\E[\sqrt{k/V}] = 0$ provided $\E[1/\sqrt{V}] < \infty$, which needs $\int_0 v^{k/2 - 3/2}\,\mathrm{d}v$ to converge at $0$, that is, $k \geq 2$; for $k = 1$ the mean is the Cauchy's, undefined. (c) $3$, $1.67$ and $1.07$: $t_3$ has standard deviation $1.73$, so its quantiles are far outside the Gaussian's; by $k = 30$ the standard deviation is $1.04$ and the quantiles are within a few percent.
\end{solution}

\begin{exercise}
\label{ex:t-mle-pivot}
Let $\Xton \iid \Gauss(\mu, \sigma^2)$ with maximum likelihood estimators $\hat{\mu} = \bar{X}_n$ and $\hat{\sigma}^2 = \frac1n\sum_i(X_i - \bar{X}_n)^2$ (\cref{ch:likelihood}). (a) Show that $\sqrt{n-1}\,(\hat{\mu} - \mu)/\hat{\sigma} \sim t_{n-1}$. (b) Propose a test of $H_0: \mu \geq 0$ against $H_1: \mu < 0$ with exact level $\alpha$ and prove its level, including for $\mu > 0$.
\end{exercise}
\begin{solution}
(a) $\hat{\sigma}^2 = \frac{n-1}{n}S_n^2$, so $\sqrt{n-1}(\hat{\mu} - \mu)/\hat{\sigma} = \sqrt{n-1}(\bar{X}_n - \mu)/\big(\sqrt{(n-1)/n}\,S_n\big) = \sqrt{n}(\bar{X}_n - \mu)/S_n \sim t_{n-1}$ by \cref{thm:t-pivot}. (b) Reject when $T = \sqrt{n-1}\,\hat{\mu}/\hat{\sigma} < -q_\alpha(t_{n-1})$. At $\mu = 0$, $T$ is the pivot and the probability is $\alpha$ by symmetry of $t_{n-1}$. For $\mu > 0$, $T = T^* + \sqrt{n-1}\,\mu/\hat{\sigma}$ with $T^* \sim t_{n-1}$ and the added term positive, so $T > T^*$ and $\Pr_\mu(T < -q) \leq \Pr(T^* < -q) = \alpha$.
\end{solution}

\begin{exercise}
\label{ex:t-coverage}
A $95\%$ Gaussian interval $\bar{X}_n \pm 1.96\,S_n/\sqrt{n}$ is computed from $n$ Gaussian observations. (a) Write its exact coverage in terms of the $t_{n-1}$ distribution and evaluate it for $n = 3, 5, 12, 30$ using \cref{fig:tdensities} (the value at $n = 3$ is $0.189$). (b) How large must $n$ be for the coverage to reach $94\%$?
\end{exercise}
\begin{solution}
(a) The interval contains $\mu$ exactly when $\abs{\sqrt{n}(\bar{X}_n - \mu)/S_n} \leq 1.96$, so the coverage is $\Pr(\abs{t_{n-1}} \leq 1.96) = 1 - \Pr(\abs{t_{n-1}} > 1.96)$: from the figure's values $0.189, 0.122, 0.076, 0.060$, the coverages are $81\%$, $88\%$, $92\%$ and $94\%$. (b) $\Pr(\abs{t_{n-1}} > 1.96) \leq 0.06$ first holds at $n = 30$ ($0.0597$); at $n = 29$ it is $0.06002$, just above.
\end{solution}

\begin{exercise}
\label{ex:t-two-sample}
A larger version of the clinical trial has $n = 70$ treated and $m = 50$ control patients, with mean decreases $156.4$ and $132.7$ and sample variances $S_1^2 = 5198.4$ and $S_2^2 = 3867.0$ (standard deviations $72.1$ and $62.2$, far larger than in the small trial). Test $H_0: \mu_1 - \mu_2 \leq 0$ against $H_1: \mu_1 - \mu_2 > 0$ at $5\%$ with Welch's test, report the one-sided $p$-value, and say what changes if the two-sided $p$-value is reported by mistake.
\end{exercise}
\begin{solution}
$T = (156.4 - 132.7)/\sqrt{5198.4/70 + 3867.0/50} = 23.7/12.31 = 1.925$. $\hat{\nu} = (74.26 + 77.34)^2/\big(74.26^2/69 + 77.34^2/49\big) = 22{,}983/(79.9 + 122.1) = 113.8$, rounded down to $113$, between $49$ and $118$. The threshold is $q_{0.05}(t_{113}) = 1.66$, so the test rejects, with one-sided $p$-value $\Pr(t_{113} > 1.925) = 0.028$; the Gaussian $p$-value is $0.027$, since at these sizes the two agree. The two-sided $p$-value is twice that, $0.057$, which would wrongly fail to reject at $5\%$: the hypothesis is one-sided, and the $p$-value must match it.
\end{solution}

\begin{exercise}
\label{ex:t-fratio}
Let $\Xton \iid \Gauss(\mu_1, \sigma_1^2)$ and $Y_1, \ldots, Y_m \iid \Gauss(\mu_2, \sigma_2^2)$ be independent samples. (a) Show that $(S_1^2/\sigma_1^2)/(S_2^2/\sigma_2^2) \sim F_{n-1,m-1}$. (b) For the boarding data ($S_1 = 2.1$, $n = 72$; $S_2 = 1.3$, $m = 56$), compute the ratio $S_1^2/S_2^2$ and explain why, with $\Pr(F_{71,55} > 2.61) < 0.001$, the pooled test was not the right choice even though it gave the same answer.
\end{exercise}
\begin{solution}
(a) By \cref{thm:cochran}, $V = (n-1)S_1^2/\sigma_1^2 \sim \chi^2_{n-1}$ and $W = (m-1)S_2^2/\sigma_2^2 \sim \chi^2_{m-1}$, independent since the samples are, and $(S_1^2/\sigma_1^2)/(S_2^2/\sigma_2^2) = (V/(n-1))/(W/(m-1))$ is $F_{n-1,m-1}$ by definition. (b) $2.1^2/1.3^2 = 2.61$. If the variances were equal this ratio would be an $F_{71,55}$ draw, and a value of $2.61$ or more happens less than once in $1{,}000$ such draws: the variances differ, and the pooled pivot's hypothesis fails. Welch's statistic is the one with a justification; here both are $27$ or more standard errors from zero, so the decision is the same.
\end{solution}

\begin{exercise}
\label{ex:t-bonferroni}
(a) For the salmon's $8{,}064$ tests at level $0.001$, compute the expected number of false rejections, the FWER assuming independence, and Bonferroni's per-test level. (b) The scanner is used on a live subject with $N = 8{,}064$ volumes of which $N_0 = 8{,}000$ have no true activity, and each test has level $0.001$. Without correction, how many false rejections are expected, and how many does Bonferroni at $\alpha = 0.05$ expect? (c) If the uncorrected tests reject $50$ volumes in all, what fraction of them are expected to be false?
\end{exercise}
\begin{solution}
(a) $8064 \times 0.001 = 8.06$; $1 - 0.999^{8064} = 0.9997$; $0.001/8064 = 1.24 \times 10^{-7}$. (b) $8000 \times 0.001 = 8$; with Bonferroni each test has level $0.05/8064 = 6.2 \times 10^{-6}$, so $8000 \times 6.2 \times 10^{-6} = 0.05$ false rejections are expected, and the FWER is at most $0.05$. (c) About $8$ of $50$, or $16\%$: the uncorrected procedure's realized false discovery proportion in this batch.
\end{solution}

\begin{exercise}
\label{ex:t-bh}
Ten tests give $p$-values $0.001, 0.008, 0.012, 0.03, 0.06, 0.2, 0.35, 0.5, 0.7, 0.9$. (a) Which tests does Bonferroni reject at $\alpha = 0.05$? (b) Which does Benjamini--Hochberg reject? (c) Suppose the fourth and fifth $p$-values were $0.022$ and $0.024$ instead of $0.03$ and $0.06$. What changes, and why does the fifth $p$-value affect whether the fourth test is rejected?
\end{exercise}
\begin{solution}
(a) The threshold is $0.005$: only the first. (b) The thresholds $i\alpha/N$ are $0.005$, $0.010$, $0.015$, $0.020$, $0.025$ and so on. The first three $p$-values are under their thresholds ($0.001 \leq 0.005$, $0.008 \leq 0.010$, $0.012 \leq 0.015$), the fourth is not ($0.03 > 0.020$), the fifth is not ($0.06 > 0.025$), and no later one is, so $i_{\max} = 3$ and the first three are rejected. (c) Now $P_{(4)} = 0.022 > 0.020$ still fails its own threshold, but $P_{(5)} = 0.024 \leq 0.025$ passes, so $i_{\max} = 5$ and the first five are rejected, the fourth included. The procedure takes the largest $i$ whose $p$-value is under the line, not the first one that fails, so a small enough fifth $p$-value pulls the fourth in with it: the small $p$-values vouch for each other.
\end{solution}
