\chapter{Exponential Families}
\label{ch:exp-families}

\begin{chaptergoals}
\begin{itemize}[nosep]
\item One form that the Bernoulli, Poisson, exponential, Gaussian and Gamma families all take, $h(y)\exp(\eta(\theta)^\top T(y) - B(\theta))$, and what it means: a base weight tilted by an exponential. Which families do not take it.
\item The canonical one-parameter form, $\exp\big((y\theta - b(\theta))/\phi + c(y, \phi)\big)$, whose one function $b$ carries the mean, the variance and the Fisher information: $\E[Y] = b'(\theta)$ and $\var(Y) = \phi\,b''(\theta)$.
\item Why the maximum likelihood estimator of the mean is the sample mean in every one of these families, why the log-likelihood is concave, and why the method of moments agreed with maximum likelihood so often.
\item Sufficiency, promised in \cref{ch:estimators}: the factorization theorem, and the sum $\sum_i T(Y_i)$ that an exponential family makes visible. Why the conjugate priors of \cref{ch:bayes-intro} exist.
\end{itemize}
\Cref{ch:regression-multi} ended with a response that is a count or a proportion, whose mean cannot be a line and whose variance moves with its mean. This chapter describes the distributions for which the regression of \cref{ch:glm-links} will work, and finds that they are the book's old families seen from one angle.
\end{chaptergoals}

\section{One form for many families}
\label{sec:ef-form}

\Cref{ch:mom-mestimation} noticed that for the Bernoulli, Poisson, exponential and Gaussian families the method of moments and maximum likelihood give the same estimator, and promised a reason.
\Cref{ch:likelihood} found that for the first three the likelihood depends on the data only through their sum, and \cref{ch:estimators} that the sum is sufficient for the kiss data.
\Cref{ch:bayes-intro} found a conjugate prior for each.
These are one fact seen four times.
Write the four PMFs and densities with every piece that depends on the parameter moved into an exponent:
\begin{align*}
\Bern(p): \quad & p^y(1-p)^{1-y} = \exp\Big(y\log\frac{p}{1-p} + \log(1 - p)\Big), && y \in \{0, 1\}; \\
\Pois(\lambda): \quad & \frac{\lambda^y e^{-\lambda}}{y!} = \frac{1}{y!}\exp\big(y\log\lambda - \lambda\big), && y = 0, 1, 2, \ldots; \\
\Expo(\lambda): \quad & \lambda e^{-\lambda y} = \exp\big(-\lambda y + \log\lambda\big), && y > 0; \\
\Gauss(\mu, \sigma^2): \quad & \frac{e^{-(y - \mu)^2/(2\sigma^2)}}{\sigma\sqrt{2\pi}} = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\exp\Big(\frac{\mu}{\sigma^2}y - \frac{1}{2\sigma^2}y^2 - \frac{\mu^2}{2\sigma^2} - \log\sigma\Big), && y \in \real.
\end{align*}
In each, the exponent is a sum of products of a function of the parameter and a function of $y$, minus a function of the parameter alone, and what is left outside depends on $y$ alone.

\begin{definition}[Exponential family]
\label{def:expfam}
A family of distributions $(\Pr_\theta)_{\theta \in \Theta}$ on $E \subset \real^q$, with $\Theta \subset \real^k$, is a \textbf{$k$-parameter exponential family} if its PMFs or densities can be written as
\[
f_\theta(y) = h(y)\exp\Big(\sum_{j=1}^k \eta_j(\theta)T_j(y) - B(\theta)\Big) = h(y)\exp\big(\eta(\theta)^\top T(y) - B(\theta)\big)
\]
for some functions $\eta_1, \ldots, \eta_k$ and $B$ of $\theta$ and $T_1, \ldots, T_k$ and $h \geq 0$ of $y$.
The $\eta_j(\theta)$ are the \textbf{natural parameters} and the $T_j(y)$ the \textbf{sufficient statistics} of the family (the name is justified in \cref{sec:ef-sufficiency}).
\end{definition}

\begin{inwords}
Every member of the family is the same base weight $h(y)$ multiplied by an exponential of a linear function of $T(y)$, and $B(\theta)$ is whatever makes the result integrate or sum to $1$: $e^{B(\theta)} = \int h(y)e^{\eta(\theta)^\top T(y)}\,\mathrm{d}y$. The parameter and the data meet only in the products $\eta_j(\theta)T_j(y)$, which is the property everything in this chapter rests on. The support, where $f_\theta > 0$, is where $h > 0$, the same for every $\theta$.

\textbf{For the kiss data.} The Bernoulli family is a one-parameter exponential family on $\{0, 1\}$ with $h(y) = 1$, $T(y) = y$, $\eta(p) = \log\big(p/(1-p)\big)$, the log-odds of \cref{ch:jeffreys}, and $B(p) = -\log(1 - p) = \log(1 + e^{\eta})$. For $124$ couples the natural parameter multiplies $\sum_i R_i = 80$.
\end{inwords}

Reading the four displays against the definition: the Poisson has $h(y) = 1/y!$, $T(y) = y$, $\eta = \log\lambda$, $B = \lambda = e^\eta$; the exponential has $h = 1$, $T(y) = y$, $\eta = -\lambda$, $B = -\log\lambda = -\log(-\eta)$; the Gaussian is a two-parameter family with $T(y) = (y, y^2)$, $\eta = (\mu/\sigma^2, -1/(2\sigma^2))$, $B = \mu^2/(2\sigma^2) + \log\sigma$ and $h = 1/\sqrt{2\pi}$, and with $\sigma^2$ known it is a one-parameter family in $\mu$ with $h(y) = e^{-y^2/(2\sigma^2)}/(\sigma\sqrt{2\pi})$.
The Gamma family $\Gammad(\alpha, \beta)$ of \cref{ch:bayes-intro}, with density $\beta^\alpha y^{\alpha - 1}e^{-\beta y}/\Gamma(\alpha)$, is a two-parameter family with $T(y) = (y, \log y)$ and $\eta = (-\beta, \alpha - 1)$; the Beta family is another (\cref{ex:ef-beta}); the binomial with a known number of trials, the geometric and the negative binomial with known size are one-parameter families (\cref{ex:ef-geometric}).

The form is more restrictive than it looks, and the restriction is where the parameter can go.

\begin{pitfall}[families that are not exponential families]
$\Unif([0, \theta])$ is not: its density is $\frac1\theta\ind(0 \leq y \leq \theta)$, and the indicator cannot be written as $h(y)e^{\eta(\theta)T(y)}$ because the support moves with $\theta$, while in an exponential family it is fixed by $h$. The Laplace family with unknown center, density $\frac12e^{-\abs{y - \mu}}$, is not: $\abs{y - \mu}$ is not a sum of products of a function of $\mu$ and a function of $y$ (\cref{ex:ef-laplace} proves this for one product; the same kind of argument handles more, and is not given here). The Student $t$ and Cauchy families are not either. The regular models of \cref{ch:mle-properties} are a wider class than the exponential families, and the uniform, which failed the regularity conditions, fails here for the same reason, its moving support.
\end{pitfall}

There is a picture behind the definition.

\begin{intuition}[one base weight, tilted]
Start from the base weight $h(y)$ and multiply it by $e^{\theta y}$, for a one-parameter family with $T(y) = y$ and natural parameter $\theta$: this \textbf{tilts} the weight, pushing mass toward large $y$ when $\theta > 0$ and toward small $y$ when $\theta < 0$, and dividing by $e^{B}$ renormalizes it.
\Cref{fig:eftilting} draws two cases.
On the left the base is the standard Gaussian density, and tilting by $e^{\theta y}$ moves the bell to the center $\theta$ without changing its shape: that is the $\Gauss(\mu, 1)$ family, with $\theta = \mu$.
On the right the base is the weight $1/y!$ on $0, 1, 2, \ldots$, and tilting by $e^{\theta y}$ gives the Poisson PMF with mean $e^\theta$.
An exponential family is the set of all exponential tilts of one base weight, and the parameter says how hard to tilt.
\end{intuition}

\caption{Exponential tilting. Left: the standard Gaussian density multiplied by $e^{\theta y}$ and renormalized, for $\theta = -1.5$ (orange, dashed), $0$ (blue) and $1.5$ (aqua, dash-dot). Right: the weights $1/y!$ tilted and renormalized, for $\theta = 0$ (blue, mean $1$) and $\theta = \log 4$ (orange, mean $4$).}

\section{The canonical form}
\label{sec:ef-canonical}

In the Bernoulli display of \cref{sec:ef-form} the parameter entered through one product, $y\log\big(p/(1-p)\big)$; write $\theta$ for that log-odds and the PMF is $\exp\big(y\theta - \log(1 + e^\theta)\big)$, one function of $\theta$ multiplying $y$ and one function of $\theta$ alone.
For one parameter and $T(y) = y$, the natural parameter itself is the best coordinate to work in, and the standard way to write the family separates out a scale.

\needspace{7\baselineskip}
\begin{definition}[Canonical exponential family]
\label{def:canonical}
A one-parameter family on $E \subset \real$ is in \textbf{canonical form} if its PMFs or densities are
\[
f_\theta(y) = \exp\Big(\frac{y\theta - b(\theta)}{\phi} + c(y, \phi)\Big), \qquad \theta \in \Theta,
\]
for known functions $b$ and $c$ and a known number $\phi > 0$, the \textbf{dispersion parameter}, with $\Theta$ an open interval on which $b$ is twice differentiable.
$\theta$ is the \textbf{canonical parameter}.
\end{definition}

\begin{inwords}
This is \cref{def:expfam} with $k = 1$, $T(y) = y$, $\eta(\theta) = \theta/\phi$, $B(\theta) = b(\theta)/\phi$ and $h(y) = e^{c(y, \phi)}$: the parameter enters only through the product $y\theta$, and one function $b$ does the normalizing. The dispersion $\phi$ is a known scale, $1$ for counts and $0/1$ data and $\sigma^2$ for a Gaussian with known variance; the family is a one-parameter family in $\theta$ with $\phi$ fixed.

\textbf{For the kiss data.} $\Bern(p)$ is canonical with $\theta = \log\big(p/(1-p)\big)$, $b(\theta) = \log(1 + e^\theta)$, $\phi = 1$ and $c = 0$: the PMF is $\exp(y\theta - \log(1 + e^\theta))$, which at $y = 1$ is $e^\theta/(1 + e^\theta) = p$. The kiss data's canonical parameter is the log-odds of turning right, estimated by $\log(80/44) = 0.598$.
\end{inwords}

The table gives the canonical forms of the book's families; each is checked by writing the PMF or density with $y\theta$ separated out, as in \cref{sec:ef-form}.

For the Gaussian, $\frac{1}{\sigma\sqrt{2\pi}}e^{-(y - \mu)^2/(2\sigma^2)} = \exp\big(\frac{y\mu - \mu^2/2}{\sigma^2} - \frac{y^2}{2\sigma^2} - \frac12\log(2\pi\sigma^2)\big)$, which is the table's row with $\theta = \mu$; the Gamma row is \cref{ex:ef-gamma}.
The canonical parameters are the scales on which this book has already found the families easiest to work with: the log-odds for a proportion, the log of a Poisson mean, the mean of a Gaussian, and, for a rate, $-\lambda$ or a multiple of it.

The payoff of the form is that one function determines the moments.

\begin{theorem}[Mean and variance from $b$]
\label{thm:ef-moments}
In a canonical exponential family,
\[
\E_\theta[Y] = b'(\theta), \qquad \var_\theta(Y) = \phi\,b''(\theta).
\]
So $b$ is convex, strictly convex wherever $\var_\theta(Y) > 0$, and $b'$ is then strictly increasing: the mean $\mu = b'(\theta)$ and the canonical parameter determine each other.
\end{theorem}
\coreproof{The density integrates to $1$ for every $\theta$. Differentiate that identity in $\theta$ once and the mean of $Y - b'(\theta)$ comes out as $0$; differentiate it twice and the variance comes out as $\phi b''(\theta)$. The same two differentiations are the score identities of \cref{ch:mle-properties}.}
\recall{The score of one observation has mean zero (\cref{prop:score-mean}), and the Fisher information is both the variance of the score and minus the expected second derivative of the log-likelihood (\cref{def:fisher}, \cref{thm:fisher-curvature}), under the regularity conditions of \cref{sec:mle-regularity}, which \cref{prop:ef-regular} below verifies for canonical families.}
\begin{proof}
Write the identity $\int \exp\big((y\theta - b(\theta))/\phi + c(y, \phi)\big)\,\mathrm{d}y = 1$ (a sum for a PMF) and differentiate in $\theta$ under the integral sign, which is allowed on the open interval $\Theta$: the integrand is a smooth function of $\theta$ and its derivatives are integrable on a neighborhood of each point (\cref{prop:ef-regular}).
The derivative of the integrand is $\frac{y - b'(\theta)}{\phi}f_\theta(y)$, so
\[
0 = \int \frac{y - b'(\theta)}{\phi}f_\theta(y)\,\mathrm{d}y = \frac{\E_\theta[Y] - b'(\theta)}{\phi},
\]
which is the mean.
Differentiating once more,
\[
0 = \int \Big(\frac{(y - b'(\theta))^2}{\phi^2} - \frac{b''(\theta)}{\phi}\Big)f_\theta(y)\,\mathrm{d}y = \frac{\var_\theta(Y)}{\phi^2} - \frac{b''(\theta)}{\phi},
\]
since $y - b'(\theta) = y - \E_\theta[Y]$, which is the variance.
A variance is non-negative, so $b'' \geq 0$ and $b$ is convex; where the variance is positive $b'' > 0$, and $b'$ is strictly increasing there.
In the language of \cref{ch:mle-properties}: the log-likelihood of one observation is $\ell(\theta) = (Y\theta - b(\theta))/\phi + c(Y, \phi)$, with score $\ell'(\theta) = (Y - b'(\theta))/\phi$ and $\ell''(\theta) = -b''(\theta)/\phi$, so the first identity is $\E_\theta[\ell'(\theta)] = 0$ and the second is $\var_\theta(\ell'(\theta)) = -\E_\theta[\ell''(\theta)]$.
\end{proof}

\Cref{fig:efb} draws $b$ for the Bernoulli family.
Its slope at $\theta$ is the mean $p = e^\theta/(1 + e^\theta)$, which rises from $0$ to $1$ as $\theta$ runs over the real line, and its curvature is the variance $p(1 - p)$, largest at $\theta = 0$ and vanishing at both ends: a straight line far out, a bend in the middle.
At the kiss data's $\hat{\theta} = 0.598$ the slope is $0.645$ and the curvature $0.229$.

The theorem says how the variance follows the mean in each family, which is the fact the regression of \cref{ch:glm-links} is built around.

\begin{definition}[Variance function]
\label{def:variance-function}
For a canonical family with means $\mu = b'(\theta)$ ranging over an interval $M$, the \textbf{variance function} is $V(\mu) = b''\big((b')^{-1}(\mu)\big)$, so that $\var(Y) = \phi\,V(\mu)$ when $\E[Y] = \mu$.
\end{definition}

\begin{inwords}
Fix the mean and the family fixes the variance, up to the dispersion. The variance function says which it is: constant for a Gaussian, equal to the mean for a Poisson, the mean squared for an exponential.

\textbf{For the kiss data.} For the Bernoulli, $b'(\theta) = e^\theta/(1 + e^\theta) = \mu$ and $b''(\theta) = \mu(1 - \mu)$, so $V(\mu) = \mu(1 - \mu)$: the variance $p(1 - p)$ that every interval for the kiss proportion has used, $0.229$ at $p = 0.645$, is the variance function at the mean.
\end{inwords}

\Cref{fig:efvariance} draws the four variance functions.
They are the reason a Gaussian linear model cannot serve for counts or proportions: it assumes $V$ constant, and for those responses $V$ moves with $\mu$.

\end{figure}

Since $b'$ is strictly increasing, it has an inverse, and the inverse is the map from the mean back to the canonical parameter.

\begin{definition}[Canonical link]
\label{def:canonical-link}
The \textbf{canonical link} of a canonical exponential family is $g_c = (b')^{-1}$, the function with $g_c(\mu) = \theta$: it sends the mean to the canonical parameter.
\end{definition}

\begin{inwords}
Each family has a scale on which its parameter enters the likelihood linearly, and the canonical link is the change of coordinates from the mean to that scale: the identity for the Gaussian, the logarithm for the Poisson, $-1/\mu$ for the exponential.

\textbf{For the kiss data.} $b'(\theta) = e^\theta/(1 + e^\theta) = \mu$ solves to $\theta = \log\big(\mu/(1 - \mu)\big)$: the canonical link of the Bernoulli family is the log-odds, which \cref{ch:glm-links} will set equal to $x^\top\beta$ to get logistic regression.
\end{inwords}

\section{Likelihood in a canonical family}
\label{sec:ef-likelihood}

The regularity conditions of \cref{ch:mle-properties} were listed and checked family by family.
In a canonical family they come for free.

\begin{proposition}[Canonical families are regular]
\label{prop:ef-regular}
A canonical exponential family with $b''(\theta) > 0$ on $\Theta$ satisfies the regularity conditions of \cref{sec:mle-regularity} at every $\theta^* \in \Theta$: the parameter is identifiable, the support does not depend on $\theta$, $\Theta$ is open, $b$ is infinitely differentiable, $f_\theta(y)$ is infinitely differentiable in $\theta$ with the derivatives movable inside the integral, and $I(\theta) = b''(\theta)/\phi > 0$.
\end{proposition}
\optionalproof{Each condition is read off the form. The one with content is moving derivatives inside the integral, which holds because $\int e^{y\theta/\phi}h(y)\,\mathrm{d}y$ is finite on an open interval, and such an integral can be differentiated under the sign as often as one likes.}
\begin{proof}
Identifiability: if $f_\theta = f_{\theta'}$ then $(\theta - \theta')y = b(\theta) - b(\theta')$ for every $y$ in the support, and the support has at least two points (one point would make $\var_\theta(Y) = 0$, against $b'' > 0$), so subtracting the equations at two of them gives $\theta = \theta'$.
The support is $E$, where $c(y, \phi)$ is finite, and does not involve $\theta$; $\Theta$ is open by definition, so $\theta^*$ is interior.
The integrand $\exp\big((y\theta - b(\theta))/\phi + c(y, \phi)\big)$ is infinitely differentiable in $\theta$.
For the differentiation under the integral sign, write $\int e^{y\theta/\phi}e^{c(y, \phi)}\,\mathrm{d}y = e^{b(\theta)/\phi}$, finite for every $\theta \in \Theta$.
The $k$th derivative of the integrand in $\theta$ is $(y/\phi)^k e^{y\theta/\phi}e^{c(y, \phi)}$.
Fix $\theta^*$ and $\delta > 0$ with $[\theta^* - 2\delta, \theta^* + 2\delta] \subset \Theta$; for $\theta$ within $\delta$ of $\theta^*$, $\abs{y/\phi}^k e^{y\theta/\phi} \leq K_k\big(e^{y(\theta^* + 2\delta)/\phi} + e^{y(\theta^* - 2\delta)/\phi}\big)$ for a constant $K_k$ depending on $k$ and $\delta$, since a power of $\abs{y}$ is eventually smaller than $e^{\delta\abs{y}/\phi}$.
The bound does not depend on $\theta$ and is integrable against $e^{c(y, \phi)}$, so the derivative passes through the integral: by the mean value theorem the difference quotient of the $(k-1)$st derivative is a $k$th derivative at a nearby $\theta$, the bound applies to it, and the dominated convergence theorem \toolkit{sec:math-limits} lets the limit inside.
Applied for $k = 1, 2, \ldots$ this makes $e^{b(\theta)/\phi}$, and so $b$, infinitely differentiable, and the derivatives of $f_\theta = e^{-b(\theta)/\phi}e^{y\theta/\phi}e^{c(y, \phi)}$ pass through the integral by the product rule.
In particular $\ell'''(\theta) = -b'''(\theta)/\phi$ does not involve $Y$ and is continuous in $\theta$, so near $\theta^*$ it is bounded by a constant, which is the third-derivative condition, and $\ell''(\theta^*) = -b''(\theta^*)/\phi$ is a constant, with variance $0$.
Finally $I(\theta) = \var_\theta(\ell'(\theta)) = \var_\theta(Y)/\phi^2 = b''(\theta)/\phi > 0$.
\end{proof}

Now take $n$ i.i.d.\ observations.
The log-likelihood is
\[
\ell_n(\theta) = \frac{\theta\sum_{i=1}^n Y_i - n\,b(\theta)}{\phi} + \sum_{i=1}^n c(Y_i, \phi),
\]
and the sample enters only through $\sum_i Y_i$, apart from the last term, which does not involve $\theta$.

\begin{theorem}[Maximum likelihood in a canonical family]
\label{thm:ef-mle}
Let $Y_1, \ldots, Y_n$ be i.i.d.\ from a canonical family with $b'' > 0$ on $\Theta$, and suppose $\bar{Y}_n$ lies in the range of $b'$.
Then:
\begin{enumerate}[nosep]
\item $\ell_n$ is strictly concave, and the maximum likelihood estimator is the unique solution of $b'(\hat{\theta}_n) = \bar{Y}_n$; the maximum likelihood estimator of the mean $\mu = b'(\theta)$ is $\hat{\mu}_n = \bar{Y}_n$;
\item $\bar{Y}_n$ is unbiased for $\mu$ with variance $\phi\,b''(\theta)/n = 1/(nI_\mu)$, where $I_\mu = 1/(\phi V(\mu))$ is the Fisher information in the mean parameterization: it attains the Cram\'er--Rao bound;
\item $\sqrt{n}(\hat{\theta}_n - \theta) \convd \Gauss\big(0, \phi/b''(\theta)\big)$ and $\sqrt{n}(\bar{Y}_n - \mu) \convd \Gauss\big(0, \phi\,b''(\theta)\big)$.
\end{enumerate}
\end{theorem}
\coreproof{The score is $(\sum_i Y_i - nb'(\theta))/\phi$, zero exactly where $b'(\theta)$ equals the sample mean, and $\ell_n'' = -nb''/\phi < 0$ everywhere. The rest is the Central Limit Theorem for $\bar{Y}_n$ and \cref{ch:mle-properties}'s theorem, whose conditions \cref{prop:ef-regular} checked.}
\recall{The Central Limit Theorem (\cref{thm:clt}): for i.i.d.\ $Y_i$ with mean $\mu$ and variance $0 < \sigma^2 < \infty$, $\sqrt{n}(\bar{Y}_n - \mu) \convd \Gauss(0, \sigma^2)$. Asymptotic normality of the MLE (\cref{thm:mle-normal}): in a regular model with $\theta^*$ interior and $I(\theta^*) > 0$, $\sqrt{n}(\hat{\theta}_n - \theta^*) \convd \Gauss(0, 1/I(\theta^*))$. The Cram\'er--Rao bound (\cref{thm:cramer-rao}): an unbiased estimator in a regular model has variance at least $1/(nI)$. Fisher information under a change of parameter (\cref{lem:fisher-reparam}): $I_\mu = I_\theta(\mathrm{d}\theta/\mathrm{d}\mu)^2$. The delta method (\cref{thm:delta}): if $\sqrt{n}(Z_n - \mu) \convd \Gauss(0, \sigma^2)$ and $g$ is continuously differentiable at $\mu$, then $\sqrt{n}(g(Z_n) - g(\mu)) \convd \Gauss(0, g'(\mu)^2\sigma^2)$.}
\begin{proof}
$\ell_n'(\theta) = \big(\sum_i Y_i - nb'(\theta)\big)/\phi$ and $\ell_n''(\theta) = -nb''(\theta)/\phi < 0$, so $\ell_n$ is strictly concave and has at most one stationary point, which is its maximum (\cref{prop:concave-max}); the stationary point is where $b'(\theta) = \bar{Y}_n$, which has a solution since $\bar{Y}_n$ is in the range of the continuous, strictly increasing $b'$, and only one.
By the invariance of maximum likelihood (\cref{prop:mle-invariance}), the MLE of $\mu = b'(\theta)$ is $b'(\hat{\theta}_n) = \bar{Y}_n$: this is (1).
For (2), $\E[\bar{Y}_n] = b'(\theta) = \mu$ and $\var(\bar{Y}_n) = \phi b''(\theta)/n$ by \cref{thm:ef-moments}.
The information in $\mu$ is $I_\mu = I_\theta(\mathrm{d}\theta/\mathrm{d}\mu)^2 = \frac{b''(\theta)}{\phi}\cdot\frac{1}{b''(\theta)^2} = \frac{1}{\phi b''(\theta)} = \frac{1}{\phi V(\mu)}$, since $\mathrm{d}\mu/\mathrm{d}\theta = b''(\theta)$, so $\var(\bar{Y}_n) = 1/(nI_\mu)$, which is the Cram\'er--Rao bound for $\mu$.
For (3), the second limit is the Central Limit Theorem with $\sigma^2 = \phi b''(\theta)$.
The first follows from the second by the delta method, since $\hat{\theta}_n = (b')^{-1}(\bar{Y}_n)$ and the derivative of $(b')^{-1}$ at $\mu$ is $1/b''(\theta)$, continuous by \cref{prop:ef-regular}, so the asymptotic variance is $\phi b''(\theta)/b''(\theta)^2 = \phi/b''(\theta)$; it agrees with \cref{thm:mle-normal} with $I(\theta) = b''(\theta)/\phi$, whose hypotheses hold by \cref{prop:ef-regular}.
\end{proof}

The condition that $\bar{Y}_n$ lie in the range of $b'$ is not empty: for the Bernoulli the range is $(0, 1)$, and a sample of all $1$s has no maximum likelihood estimate on $\Theta = \real$, the log-likelihood $n\theta - n\log(1 + e^\theta)$ increasing forever.
This is the problem that will return, with predictors, in \cref{ch:glm-links}.

This is the explanation \cref{ch:mom-mestimation} promised.
In a canonical family the likelihood equation is $b'(\theta) = \bar{Y}_n$, that is, `set the model's mean equal to the sample mean', which is exactly the method of moments; and for the uniform and Laplace families, which are not exponential families, the two methods part.
With $k$ natural parameters the same differentiation gives the likelihood equations $\E_\eta[T_j(Y)] = \frac1n\sum_i T_j(Y_i)$ for $j = 1, \ldots, k$ (in the natural parameterization, with $B$ a function of $\eta$: differentiating $\int h(y)e^{\eta^\top T(y) - B(\eta)}\,\mathrm{d}y = 1$ in $\eta_j$ gives $\partial B/\partial\eta_j = \E_\eta[T_j(Y)]$ as in \cref{thm:ef-moments}, and $\partial\ell_n/\partial\eta_j = \sum_i T_j(Y_i) - n\,\partial B/\partial\eta_j$), which match the moments of the sufficient statistics; for the Gaussian with both parameters unknown, $T = (Y, Y^2)$, and the equations match the first two sample moments, which is how \cref{ch:mom-mestimation} found $\hat{\mu}$ and $\hat{\sigma}^2$.

\begin{bridge}[the sample mean is the best unbiased estimator]
An introductory course states, without proof, that the sample mean is the best unbiased estimator of a population mean. Part (2) of \cref{thm:ef-mle} is the statement with its conditions: for a sample from a canonical exponential family, $\bar{Y}_n$ is unbiased for the mean and its variance $\phi b''(\theta)/n$ is the Cram\'er--Rao bound, which no unbiased estimator can beat. Outside these families the claim can fail: for the Laplace family of \cref{ch:mom-mestimation} the sample median has asymptotic variance $1$ against the sample mean's $2$, and for $\Unif([0, \theta])$ the largest observation, rescaled by $(n+1)/n$ to remove its bias, has error of order $1/n$ against the $1/\sqrt{n}$ of the estimator $2\bar{Y}_n$ built from the mean (\cref{ch:mle-properties}).
\end{bridge}

Here are the three running data sets on their canonical scales.

\begin{worked}[three estimates on the canonical scale]
For the kiss data, $b'(\theta) = e^\theta/(1 + e^\theta) = 0.645$ gives $\hat{\theta} = \log(80/44) = 0.598$, the log-odds, with $I(\hat{\theta}) = b''(\hat{\theta}) = 0.645 \times 0.355 = 0.229$ and standard error $1/\sqrt{124 \times 0.229} = 0.188$, the number the odds-ratio bridge of \cref{ch:limit-tools} reached by the delta method; $0.598 \pm 1.96 \times 0.188$, exponentiated, is the odds interval $[1.26, 2.63]$ found there. For the siblings data of \cref{ch:models}, $24$ values of $X_i - 1$ summing to $24$, the Poisson mean is $\hat{\lambda} = 1$, so $\hat{\theta} = \log 1 = 0$ with $I = b''(0) = e^0 = 1$ and standard error $1/\sqrt{24} = 0.204$. For the subway's $64$ waits averaging $6.23$ minutes, the exponential's $b'(\theta) = -1/\theta = 6.23$ gives $\hat{\theta} = -0.1605$ per minute, which is $-\hat{\lambda}$, with $b''(\hat\theta) = 1/\hat\theta^2 = 38.8$ and standard error $\sqrt{1/(64 \times 38.8)} = 0.020$.
\end{worked}

The kiss log-likelihood is concave in $p$ and in $\theta$, but in $\theta$ it is close to the parabola $-\frac12 n b''(\hat{\theta})(\theta - \hat{\theta})^2$ over the range drawn, which is what makes the Gaussian approximation to $\hat{\theta}$ accurate; in $p$ the curve is lopsided against its parabola, steep toward $1$ and gentle toward $0$.
\Cref{ch:wald-lrt}, which found that Wald's test depends on the parameterization, was looking at the same fact.

The kiss standard error $0.188$ on the log-odds scale came out of $1/\sqrt{nb''(\hat{\theta})} = 1/\sqrt{124 \times 0.229}$ here and out of $\sqrt{1/80 + 1/44}$ in \cref{ch:limit-tools}. \end{sanitycheck}

\section{Sufficiency}
\label{sec:ef-sufficiency}

\Cref{ch:estimators} defined a sufficient statistic, a function $T$ of the sample such that the conditional distribution of the sample given $T$ is free of $\theta$, showed that the count of right turns is sufficient for the kiss data, and promised the theory would reappear here.
Checking the definition directly, as that chapter did, means computing a conditional distribution.
A criterion that reads sufficiency off the likelihood is easier.

\needspace{8\baselineskip}
\begin{theorem}[Factorization theorem]
\label{thm:factorization}
Let the sample $\mathbf{Y} = (Y_1, \ldots, Y_n)$ have joint PMF or density $f_\theta(\mathbf{y})$.
A statistic $T(\mathbf{Y})$ is sufficient for $\theta$ if and only if
\[
f_\theta(\mathbf{y}) = g_\theta\big(T(\mathbf{y})\big)\,k(\mathbf{y})
\]
for some functions $g_\theta$ of the statistic's value and $k$ of the sample alone.
\end{theorem}
\coreproof{For a discrete sample, the conditional probability of $\mathbf{y}$ given $T = t$ is $f_\theta(\mathbf{y})$ divided by the sum of $f_\theta$ over the samples with $T = t$; if $f_\theta$ factors, the $g_\theta(t)$ cancels top and bottom. Conversely, if the conditional probability is free of $\theta$, it is the $k$.}
\begin{proof}
Take a discrete sample space (the continuous case replaces sums by integrals, and its rigorous version needs more measure theory than this book develops).
Fix $t$ and a sample $\mathbf{y}$ with $T(\mathbf{y}) = t$.
Then
\[
\Pr_\theta(\mathbf{Y} = \mathbf{y} \mid T = t) = \frac{\Pr_\theta(\mathbf{Y} = \mathbf{y})}{\Pr_\theta(T = t)} = \frac{f_\theta(\mathbf{y})}{\sum_{\mathbf{y}' : T(\mathbf{y}') = t} f_\theta(\mathbf{y}')},
\]
since $\{\mathbf{Y} = \mathbf{y}\}$ is contained in $\{T = t\}$.
If $f_\theta = g_\theta(T)\,k$, the right side is $g_\theta(t)k(\mathbf{y})\big/\big(g_\theta(t)\sum_{\mathbf{y}'}k(\mathbf{y}')\big) = k(\mathbf{y})/\sum_{\mathbf{y}' : T(\mathbf{y}') = t}k(\mathbf{y}')$, free of $\theta$: $T$ is sufficient.
Conversely, if $T$ is sufficient, the conditional probability is a function $k(\mathbf{y})$ free of $\theta$, and $f_\theta(\mathbf{y}) = \Pr_\theta(T = t)\,k(\mathbf{y})$ with $g_\theta(t) = \Pr_\theta(T = t)$.
\end{proof}

\begin{worked}[four sufficient statistics by factorization]
Kiss data: $f_p(\mathbf{r}) = p^{s}(1 - p)^{n - s}$ with $s = \sum_i r_i$, which is $g_p(s)$ times $k = 1$, so $S$ is sufficient, as \cref{ch:estimators} found by computing the conditional distribution. Gaussian with both parameters unknown: the joint density is $(2\pi\sigma^2)^{-n/2}\exp\big(-\frac{1}{2\sigma^2}\sum_i y_i^2 + \frac{\mu}{\sigma^2}\sum_i y_i - \frac{n\mu^2}{2\sigma^2}\big)$, a function of $(\sum_i y_i, \sum_i y_i^2)$ and the parameters, so the pair $(\sum_i Y_i, \sum_i Y_i^2)$, equivalently $(\bar{Y}_n, S_n^2)$, is sufficient for $(\mu, \sigma^2)$: the $t$-test of \cref{ch:t-test} used nothing else. Uniform $[0, \theta]$: the joint density is $\theta^{-n}\ind(\max_i y_i \leq \theta)\ind(\min_i y_i \geq 0)$, a function of $\max_i y_i$ and $\theta$ times a function of the sample, so the largest observation is sufficient; the MLE of \cref{ch:likelihood} is a function of it, as the theorem says every MLE must be, since maximizing $g_\theta(T(\mathbf{y}))\,k(\mathbf{y})$ in $\theta$ uses the sample only through $T$. Laplace with unknown center: $2^{-n}\exp(-\sum_i\abs{y_i - \mu})$ does not change when the observations are reordered, so the sorted sample $(Y_{(1)}, \ldots, Y_{(n)})$ is sufficient, and the exponent does not split into a function of $\mu$ times a function of one statistic (\cref{ex:ef-laplace}).
\end{worked}

In an exponential family the factorization is on the surface.

\begin{corollary}[Sufficiency in an exponential family]
\label{cor:ef-sufficient}
For $Y_1, \ldots, Y_n$ i.i.d.\ from a $k$-parameter exponential family, the vector of sums $\sum_{i=1}^n T(Y_i) = \big(\sum_i T_1(Y_i), \ldots, \sum_i T_k(Y_i)\big)$ is sufficient for $\theta$.
In a canonical family, $\sum_i Y_i$ is sufficient.
\end{corollary}
\optionalproof{Multiply the $n$ factors: the exponents add, and the parameter meets the data only through the sums.}
\begin{proof}
$\prod_i f_\theta(y_i) = \big(\prod_i h(y_i)\big)\exp\big(\eta(\theta)^\top\sum_i T(y_i) - nB(\theta)\big)$, which is $k(\mathbf{y}) = \prod_i h(y_i)$ times a function of $\theta$ and $\sum_i T(y_i)$.
\end{proof}

A $k$-parameter exponential family thus has a sufficient statistic of fixed dimension $k$ however large $n$ is: $124$ couples reduce to one count, $64$ waits to one sum, a Gaussian sample of any size to a sum and a sum of squares.
The converse is a classical theorem, due to Darmois, Koopman and Pitman (1935 to 1936): among families with a fixed support and smooth densities, the ones whose i.i.d.\ samples admit a sufficient statistic of bounded dimension are exactly the exponential families.
It is stated without proof; nothing later depends on it.
The sufficiency principle of \cref{ch:estimators} then says what it has said all along: every method for the kiss data is a function of $80$ and $124$, and every method for a Gaussian sample is a function of $\bar{Y}_n$ and $S_n^2$, not because those were convenient choices but because nothing else in the sample speaks about the parameter.

\begin{remark}[Rao, 1945, and Blackwell, 1947]
Sufficiency improves estimators as well as summarizing data.
If $T$ is sufficient and $W$ is any unbiased estimator of $\theta$, then $\E[W \mid T]$ is a function of $T$ alone (the conditional distribution given $T$ does not involve $\theta$), still unbiased by the tower property, and with variance no larger than $W$'s: both have mean $\theta$, and $\E\big[\E[W \mid T]^2\big] \leq \E[W^2]$ \toolkit{sec:math-condexp}, so $\var(\E[W \mid T]) \leq \var(W)$.
For the kiss data, $W = R_1$, the first couple's turn, is unbiased for $p$ with variance $p(1 - p)$, and $\E[R_1 \mid S] = S/n$, the sample proportion, with variance $p(1 - p)/n$: conditioning on the count recovers $\hat{p}$ from a single observation.
\end{remark}

\section{Conjugate priors, explained}
\label{sec:ef-conjugate}

\Cref{ch:bayes-intro} found a conjugate family of priors for each of its models and defined conjugacy as the posterior staying in the prior's family.
Written on the canonical scale, every one of those families has the same shape as the likelihood.

\begin{proposition}[Conjugate priors for a canonical family]
\label{prop:ef-conjugate}
For a canonical family with dispersion $\phi$, let the prior density of $\theta$ be
\[
\pi(\theta) \propto \exp\Big(\frac{m_0\theta - n_0\,b(\theta)}{\phi}\Big), \qquad \theta \in \Theta,
\]
for numbers $m_0$ and $n_0 > 0$ that make it integrable.
Then after $n$ i.i.d.\ observations with sum $\sum_i Y_i$, the posterior is of the same form with $(m_0, n_0)$ replaced by $(m_0 + \sum_i Y_i, n_0 + n)$.
\end{proposition}
\optionalproof{Prior times likelihood: both are exponentials of a linear function of $\theta$ minus a multiple of $b(\theta)$, and exponents add.}
\begin{proof}
$\pi(\theta)L_n(\mathbf{y}; \theta) \propto \exp\big((m_0\theta - n_0b(\theta))/\phi\big)\exp\big((\theta\sum_i y_i - nb(\theta))/\phi\big) = \exp\big(((m_0 + \sum_i y_i)\theta - (n_0 + n)b(\theta))/\phi\big)$, the prior's form with the new numbers.
\end{proof}

The prior behaves like $n_0$ earlier observations whose sum was $m_0$, which is the `imaginary couples' reading of \cref{ch:bayes-intro} made general.
For the Bernoulli, with $\theta$ the log-odds and $b(\theta) = \log(1 + e^\theta)$, the prior $\exp(m_0\theta - n_0\log(1 + e^\theta)) = p^{m_0}(1 - p)^{n_0 - m_0}$ on the scale of $\theta$ is, after the change of variables $\mathrm{d}\theta = \mathrm{d}p/(p(1-p))$ of \cref{ch:jeffreys}, the density $p^{m_0 - 1}(1 - p)^{n_0 - m_0 - 1}$ on the scale of $p$: the $\Betad(m_0, n_0 - m_0)$ prior, updated to $\Betad(m_0 + S, n_0 - m_0 + n - S)$, which is \cref{thm:beta-bernoulli} with $a = m_0$ and $b = n_0 - m_0$.
The Gamma prior for a Poisson mean and for an exponential rate, and the Gaussian prior for a Gaussian mean, are the same proposition in the other rows of the table (\cref{ex:ef-conjugate}).

\section{What comes next}
\label{sec:ef-next}

A canonical family has one parameter and $n$ observations that share it.
\Cref{ch:glm-links} lets each observation have its own $\theta_i$, tied to that observation's predictors by $g(\mu_i) = x_i^\top\beta$ for a link $g$, which is the generalized linear model: the Gaussian family with the identity link gives back \cref{ch:regression-multi}, the Bernoulli family with its canonical link, the log-odds, gives logistic regression, and the Poisson family with the log link gives regression for counts.
The log-likelihood in $\beta$ stays concave under the canonical link, the maximum likelihood estimator has no closed form but can be found by Newton's method, and the large-sample theory of \cref{ch:mle-properties}, verified here for canonical families, carries it through to tests and intervals.

\section{Summary}
\label{sec:ef-summary}

\begin{itemize}[nosep]
\item A $k$-parameter exponential family has densities $h(y)\exp(\eta(\theta)^\top T(y) - B(\theta))$: the base weight $h$ tilted by an exponential, with a fixed support. Bernoulli, Poisson, exponential, Gaussian, Gamma and Beta are; the uniform (moving support), Laplace with unknown center, $t$ and Cauchy are not.
\item Canonical form: $\exp\big((y\theta - b(\theta))/\phi + c(y, \phi)\big)$, with $\theta$ the canonical parameter and $\phi$ the known dispersion. Kiss: $\theta$ is the log-odds and $b = \log(1 + e^\theta)$; Poisson: $\log\lambda$, $e^\theta$; exponential: $-\lambda$, $-\log(-\theta)$; Gaussian with $\sigma^2$ known: $\mu$, $\theta^2/2$, $\phi = \sigma^2$.
\item $\E[Y] = b'(\theta)$, $\var(Y) = \phi\,b''(\theta)$, so $b$ is convex and $b'$ strictly increasing; the variance function $V(\mu) = b''((b')^{-1}(\mu))$ is $1$, $\mu$, $\mu^2$, $\mu(1 - \mu)$ for the four families; the canonical link is $g_c = (b')^{-1}$, the log-odds for the Bernoulli.
\item Canonical families are regular. For $n$ observations $\ell_n$ is strictly concave, the MLE solves $b'(\hat{\theta}_n) = \bar{Y}_n$ (so it is the method of moments), $\bar{Y}_n$ attains the Cram\'er--Rao bound for the mean, $I(\theta) = b''(\theta)/\phi$, and $\sqrt{n}(\hat{\theta}_n - \theta) \convd \Gauss(0, \phi/b''(\theta))$. Kiss: $\hat{\theta} = 0.598$, standard error $0.188$, odds interval $[1.26, 2.63]$.
\item Factorization theorem: $T$ is sufficient exactly when $f_\theta(\mathbf{y}) = g_\theta(T(\mathbf{y}))k(\mathbf{y})$. In an exponential family $\sum_i T(Y_i)$ is sufficient, of dimension $k$ for every $n$; Darmois, Koopman and Pitman: only exponential families have that (stated). Rao and Blackwell: conditioning an unbiased estimator on a sufficient statistic never raises its variance.
\item The prior $\exp((m_0\theta - n_0b(\theta))/\phi)$ is conjugate for every canonical family, updating to $(m_0 + \sum_i Y_i, n_0 + n)$: the Beta, Gamma and Gaussian conjugates of \cref{ch:bayes-intro} are its rows.
\end{itemize}

\section{Exercises}
\label{sec:ef-exercises}

\begin{exercise}
\label{ex:ef-gamma}
(a) Write the $\Gammad(\alpha, \beta)$ density with $\alpha$ known in canonical form and check the table's row: $\theta = -\beta/\alpha$, $b(\theta) = -\log(-\theta)$, $\phi = 1/\alpha$. (b) Use \cref{thm:ef-moments} to find the mean and variance, and the variance function. (c) Which row of the table is the case $\alpha = 1$?
\end{exercise}
\begin{solution}
(a) $\frac{\beta^\alpha}{\Gamma(\alpha)}y^{\alpha-1}e^{-\beta y} = \exp\big(-\beta y + \alpha\log\beta + (\alpha - 1)\log y - \log\Gamma(\alpha)\big)$. With $\theta = -\beta/\alpha$ and $\phi = 1/\alpha$, $-\beta y = y\theta/\phi$ and $\alpha\log\beta = \alpha\log(-\theta) + \alpha\log\alpha = -b(\theta)/\phi + \alpha\log\alpha$, so the exponent is $(y\theta - b(\theta))/\phi + \alpha\log\alpha + (\alpha - 1)\log y - \log\Gamma(\alpha)$, and $c(y, \phi) = \alpha\log(\alpha y) - \log y - \log\Gamma(\alpha)$. (b) $b'(\theta) = -1/\theta = \alpha/\beta$ and $b''(\theta) = 1/\theta^2 = \alpha^2/\beta^2$, so $\E[Y] = \alpha/\beta$ and $\var(Y) = \phi b'' = \alpha/\beta^2$, the Gamma's mean and variance of \cref{def:gamma}; $V(\mu) = \mu^2$, with dispersion $1/\alpha$. (c) $\alpha = 1$ is $\Expo(\beta)$: $\theta = -\beta$, $\phi = 1$, $c = 0$.
\end{solution}

\begin{exercise}
\label{ex:ef-geometric}
(a) Show that $\Bin(m, p)$ with $m$ known is a canonical family with $\theta$ the log-odds, $b(\theta) = m\log(1 + e^\theta)$ and $c(y) = \log\binom{m}{y}$, and recover its mean and variance from $b$. (b) Show that the geometric family $\Pr(Y = y) = (1 - p)^{y}p$, $y = 0, 1, 2, \ldots$, is canonical with $\theta = \log(1 - p)$ and $b(\theta) = -\log(1 - e^\theta)$, and compute $\E[Y]$ from $b'$. (c) Show that the negative binomial with known size $r$, $\Pr(Y = y) = \binom{y + r - 1}{y}(1 - p)^yp^r$, is canonical with the same $\theta$ and $b(\theta) = -r\log(1 - e^\theta)$.
\end{exercise}
\begin{solution}
(a) $\binom{m}{y}p^y(1-p)^{m-y} = \binom{m}{y}\exp\big(y\log\frac{p}{1-p} + m\log(1 - p)\big)$, and $m\log(1 - p) = -m\log(1 + e^\theta)$. Then $b'(\theta) = me^\theta/(1 + e^\theta) = mp$ and $b''(\theta) = mp(1 - p)$. (b) $(1 - p)^yp = \exp\big(y\log(1 - p) + \log p\big)$ with $\log p = \log(1 - e^\theta)$, so $b(\theta) = -\log(1 - e^\theta)$ on $\theta < 0$; $b'(\theta) = e^\theta/(1 - e^\theta) = (1 - p)/p$, the geometric mean. (c) $\log p^r = -r\log(1 - e^\theta)$, so $b(\theta) = -r\log(1 - e^\theta)$ with $c(y) = \log\binom{y + r - 1}{y}$, and $b'(\theta) = re^\theta/(1 - e^\theta) = r(1 - p)/p$, $r$ times the geometric mean.
\end{solution}

\begin{exercise}
\label{ex:ef-beta}
Show that the Beta family $\Betad(a, b)$, with density $p^{a-1}(1 - p)^{b-1}/B(a, b)$ on $(0, 1)$, is a two-parameter exponential family, and name $\eta$, $T$, $B$ and $h$.
\end{exercise}
\begin{solution}
$p^{a-1}(1-p)^{b-1}/B(a, b) = \exp\big((a - 1)\log p + (b - 1)\log(1 - p) - \log B(a, b)\big)$: $T(p) = (\log p, \log(1 - p))$, $\eta = (a - 1, b - 1)$, the normalizer is $\log B(a, b)$, the log of the Beta function, and $h = 1$ on $(0, 1)$.
\end{solution}

\begin{exercise}
\label{ex:ef-laplace}
Show that the Laplace family with densities $\frac12e^{-\abs{y - \mu}}$, $\mu \in \real$, is not a one-parameter exponential family. (Suppose $\abs{y - \mu} = \eta(\mu)T(y) + B(\mu) + a(y)$ for all $y$ and $\mu$, and look at what the equation says for $y$ far to the right of two fixed centers.)
\end{exercise}
\begin{solution}
Suppose $\abs{y - \mu} = \eta(\mu)T(y) + B(\mu) + a(y)$ for all $y, \mu$. Pick $\mu_1 < \mu_2$ with $\eta(\mu_1) \neq \eta(\mu_2)$ (if $\eta$ were constant, $\eta T(y)$ could be absorbed into $a(y)$, and then $\abs{y - \mu_1} - \abs{y - \mu_2} = B(\mu_1) - B(\mu_2)$ would be constant in $y$ for any two centers; but it equals $\mu_2 - \mu_1$ to the right of both and $\mu_1 - \mu_2$ to the left). Subtracting the equations for $\mu_1$ and $\mu_2$, $\abs{y - \mu_1} - \abs{y - \mu_2} = (\eta(\mu_1) - \eta(\mu_2))T(y) + \text{constant}$, and the left side equals $\mu_2 - \mu_1$ for every $y \geq \mu_2$; so $T$ is constant on $[\mu_2, \infty)$. Then for every $\mu$, $\abs{y - \mu} - a(y) = \eta(\mu)T(y) + B(\mu)$ is constant in $y$ on $[\mu_2, \infty)$, so for two centers $\mu_2 < \mu < \mu'$ the difference $\abs{y - \mu} - \abs{y - \mu'}$ is constant in $y$ on $[\mu_2, \infty)$; but between $\mu$ and $\mu'$ it equals $2y - \mu - \mu'$, which is not constant. No such representation exists.
\end{solution}

\begin{exercise}
\label{ex:ef-variance-determines}
The variance function determines $b$, and with it the mean, the variance and the canonical link. (a) Show that if a canonical family with $\phi = 1$ has $V(\mu) = \mu$ on $(0, \infty)$, then $b'' = b'$, so $b'(\theta) = Ce^\theta$, and with the normalization $C = 1$ this is the Poisson row's $b$. (b) Which rows of the table have $V(\mu) = \mu(1 - \mu)$ and $V(\mu) = \mu^2$?
\end{exercise}
\begin{solution}
(a) $V(\mu) = b''(\theta)$ at $\mu = b'(\theta)$, so $b''(\theta) = b'(\theta)$; the solutions are $b'(\theta) = Ce^\theta$, and $C = e^{\theta_0}$ only shifts $\theta$ by $\theta_0$, so take $b' = e^\theta$ and $b = e^\theta$ up to a constant, which is absorbed in $c$: the Poisson row's $b$. (b) $b'' = b'(1 - b')$ gives $b' = e^\theta/(1 + e^\theta)$ up to a shift of $\theta$, the Bernoulli row's $b$; $b'' = (b')^2$ gives $b' = -1/\theta$ up to a shift, the exponential row's, and with dispersion $1/\alpha$ the Gamma row's. (The base weight $e^{c(y)}$ is then forced as well, since $\int e^{y\theta}e^{c(y)}\,\mathrm{d}y = e^{b(\theta)}$ for every $\theta$ determines it; that a weight is determined by this transform is not proved in this book.)
\end{solution}

\begin{exercise}
\label{ex:ef-cr-attained}
For the kiss data, the sample proportion attains the Cram\'er--Rao bound for $p$ (\cref{ch:mle-properties}). (a) Show from \cref{thm:ef-mle} that the same holds for the Poisson mean, the exponential mean and the Gaussian mean with known variance. (b) The MLE of the exponential rate, $\hat{\lambda} = 1/\bar{T}_n$, has expectation $\frac{n}{n-1}\lambda$ (\cref{ch:estimators}). Explain why neither it nor its unbiased correction $(n-1)/\sum_i T_i$ attains the bound for $\lambda$ at any finite $n$, and why this does not contradict (a). (The bound is attained by an unbiased $W$ exactly when the score is $a(\theta)(W - \tau(\theta))$ for some $a$, the equality case of the Cauchy--Schwarz step in its proof.)
\end{exercise}
\begin{solution}
(a) In each family the mean is $b'(\theta)$ and $\bar{Y}_n$ is unbiased with variance $\phi b''(\theta)/n = 1/(nI_\mu)$ by part (2) of the theorem. (b) $\hat{\lambda}$ is biased, so the bound, which is for unbiased estimators, does not apply to it. The score of the exponential sample is $n/\lambda - \sum_i T_i$, an affine function of $\bar{T}_n$, so it is an affine function of $W - \tau$ only for $W$ affine in $\bar{T}_n$; $(n - 1)/\sum_i T_i$ is not, so it does not attain the bound (its variance is $\lambda^2/(n - 2)$ for $n > 2$, against the bound $\lambda^2/n$). No contradiction: (a) is about the mean $1/\lambda$, and in a canonical family the bound is attained for the mean and for no parameter that is not an affine function of it: the score $(\sum_i Y_i - nb'(\theta))/\phi$ is affine in $\bar{Y}_n$, so an estimator $W$ free of $\theta$ with $W - \tau(\theta) = c(\theta)\,\ell_n'(\theta)$ for every $\theta$ must be $\alpha\bar{Y}_n + \beta$ with constants $\alpha, \beta$, which forces $\tau = \alpha b'(\theta) + \beta$.
\end{solution}

\begin{exercise}
\label{ex:ef-sufficient-stats}
By the factorization theorem, find a sufficient statistic for (a) $\theta$ in $\Unif([\theta, \theta + 1])$; (b) $(\alpha, \beta)$ in the Gamma family; (c) $\lambda$ in the Poisson family, and show that $\bar{Y}_n$ and $\sum_i Y_i$ are each sufficient while $Y_1$ alone is not.
\end{exercise}
\begin{solution}
(a) The joint density is $\ind(\theta \leq \min_i y_i)\,\ind(\max_i y_i \leq \theta + 1)$, a function of the pair $(\min_i y_i, \max_i y_i)$ and $\theta$: the two extremes together are sufficient. (b) The joint density is
\[
\prod_{i=1}^n \frac{\beta^\alpha}{\Gamma(\alpha)}y_i^{\alpha - 1}e^{-\beta y_i} = \frac{\beta^{n\alpha}}{\Gamma(\alpha)^n}\Big(\prod_i y_i\Big)^{\alpha - 1}e^{-\beta\sum_i y_i},
\]
so $(\sum_i Y_i, \prod_i Y_i)$, or equivalently $(\sum_i Y_i, \sum_i\log Y_i)$, is sufficient, as \cref{cor:ef-sufficient} says with $T = (y, \log y)$. (c) $\prod_i \lambda^{y_i}e^{-\lambda}/y_i! = \lambda^{\sum_i y_i}e^{-n\lambda}/\prod_i y_i!$, so $\sum_i Y_i$ is sufficient, and so is $\bar{Y}_n$, a one-to-one function of it. $Y_1$ is not: given $Y_1 = y_1$ the remaining $Y_2, \ldots, Y_n$ still have the $\Pois(\lambda)$ distribution, which depends on $\lambda$.
\end{solution}

\begin{exercise}
\label{ex:ef-conjugate}
Apply \cref{prop:ef-conjugate} to (a) the Poisson family, showing that the conjugate prior on the scale of $\lambda = e^\theta$ is $\Gammad(m_0, n_0)$ and the posterior $\Gammad(m_0 + \sum_i Y_i, n_0 + n)$; (b) the Gaussian family with $\sigma^2$ known, showing that the prior is $\Gauss(m_0/n_0, \sigma^2/n_0)$ and the posterior mean is $(m_0 + \sum_i Y_i)/(n_0 + n)$, the precision-weighted average of \cref{ch:bayes-intro}; (c) the $\Expo(\lambda)$ family, showing that the conjugate prior on the scale of $\lambda = -\theta$ is $\Gammad(n_0 + 1, m_0)$ and saying what $\alpha$ and $\beta$ in \cref{ch:bayes-intro}'s $\Gammad(\alpha, \beta)$ prior for the subway rate count as.
\end{exercise}
\begin{solution}
(a) $\pi(\theta) \propto \exp(m_0\theta - n_0e^\theta)$, and with $\lambda = e^\theta$, $\mathrm{d}\theta = \mathrm{d}\lambda/\lambda$, the density of $\lambda$ is proportional to $\lambda^{m_0 - 1}e^{-n_0\lambda}$: $\Gammad(m_0, n_0)$, updated to $\Gammad(m_0 + \sum_i Y_i, n_0 + n)$, the Gamma--Poisson pair of \cref{ch:bayes-intro}. (b) $\pi(\theta) \propto \exp\big((m_0\theta - n_0\theta^2/2)/\sigma^2\big) = \exp\big(-\frac{n_0}{2\sigma^2}(\theta - m_0/n_0)^2\big)$ up to a constant: $\Gauss(m_0/n_0, \sigma^2/n_0)$, a prior worth $n_0$ observations averaging $m_0/n_0$. The posterior is $\Gauss\big((m_0 + \sum_i Y_i)/(n_0 + n), \sigma^2/(n_0 + n)\big)$, whose mean weights the prior mean by $n_0$ and the sample mean by $n$, that is, by the precisions $n_0/\sigma^2$ and $n/\sigma^2$ of \cref{ch:bayes-intro}. (c) With $\theta = -\lambda$ and $b(\theta) = -\log(-\theta) = -\log\lambda$, $\pi(\theta) \propto \exp(-m_0\lambda + n_0\log\lambda) = \lambda^{n_0}e^{-m_0\lambda}$, and $\mathrm{d}\theta = -\mathrm{d}\lambda$ changes nothing but the sign, so the prior on $\lambda$ is $\Gammad(n_0 + 1, m_0)$: $\alpha = n_0 + 1$ and $\beta = m_0$. \Cref{ch:bayes-intro} read the prior as $\alpha$ waits lasting $\beta$ minutes, since the data add $n$ to $\alpha$ and $\sum_i T_i$ to $\beta$; on the canonical scale the count is $n_0 = \alpha - 1$, one fewer, because the flat prior on $\lambda$, which is $\Gammad(1, 0)$, counts as no waits at all. The posterior is $\Gammad(n_0 + n + 1, m_0 + \sum_i T_i) = \Gammad(\alpha + n, \beta + \sum_i T_i)$, the update of \cref{ch:bayes-intro}.
\end{solution}

\begin{exercise}
\label{ex:ef-rao-blackwell}
For the siblings data modeled as $Y_i = X_i - 1 \sim \Pois(\lambda)$, the statistic $W = \ind(Y_1 = 0)$ is unbiased for $e^{-\lambda}$, the probability of having no siblings. (a) Show that $\E[W \mid S] = (1 - 1/n)^S$ for $S = \sum_i Y_i$. (Given $S = s$, $Y_1 \sim \Bin(s, 1/n)$.) (b) Compute both estimates for the $24$ values with $S = 24$, and compare with the plug-in $e^{-\hat{\lambda}}$.
\end{exercise}
\begin{solution}
(a) A sum of independent Poissons is Poisson with the summed mean (its PMF is the convolution, which collapses by the binomial theorem), so $Y_2 + \cdots + Y_n \sim \Pois((n-1)\lambda)$ and $S \sim \Pois(n\lambda)$, and
\begin{align*}
\Pr(Y_1 = y \mid S = s) &= \frac{\Pr(Y_1 = y)\Pr(Y_2 + \cdots + Y_n = s - y)}{\Pr(S = s)} \\
&= \frac{e^{-\lambda}\lambda^y}{y!}\cdot\frac{e^{-(n-1)\lambda}((n-1)\lambda)^{s-y}}{(s-y)!}\cdot\frac{s!}{e^{-n\lambda}(n\lambda)^s} = \binom{s}{y}\Big(\frac1n\Big)^y\Big(1 - \frac1n\Big)^{s-y},
\end{align*}
the $\Bin(s, 1/n)$ PMF, free of $\lambda$; so $\Pr(Y_1 = 0 \mid S = s) = (1 - 1/n)^s$. (b) The first student in \cref{ch:models}'s list has $X_1 = 2$, so $Y_1 = 1$ and $W = 0$: an unbiased estimate of $e^{-\lambda}$ that uses one person. The Rao--Blackwell estimate is $(1 - 1/24)^{24} = 0.360$, against the plug-in $e^{-1} = 0.368$ and the raw proportion of only children, $8/24 = 0.333$. The Rao--Blackwell estimate is unbiased, the plug-in is the MLE by invariance and is not, and they agree to two decimals here.
\end{solution}

\begin{exercise}
\label{ex:ef-concave-p}
\Cref{fig:efkissloglik} drew the kiss log-likelihood on two scales. (a) Compute the third derivative of $\ell_n$ in $\theta$ and in $p$ at the maximum, for $n = 124$ and $\hat{p} = 0.645$, and explain which scale the parabola fits better. (b) Wald's test of \cref{ch:wald-lrt} in $\theta$ uses $\hat{\theta}^2/\widehat{\var}(\hat{\theta})$; compute it for $H_0: \theta = 0$ and compare with Wald's statistic $11.41$ in $p$.
\end{exercise}
\begin{solution}
(a) In $\theta$: $\ell_n'''(\theta) = -nb'''(\theta) = -np(1 - p)(1 - 2p)$, which is $-124 \times 0.229 \times (-0.29) = 8.2$ at the maximum; in $p$: $\ell_n(p) = S\log p + (n - S)\log(1 - p)$ has $\ell_n''' = 2S/p^3 - 2(n - S)/(1 - p)^3$, which is $2 \times 80/0.645^3 - 2 \times 44/0.355^3 = 596 - 1{,}967 = -1{,}371$ (with the rounded $\hat{p}$; $80/124$ gives $-1{,}374$). The two numbers are in different units, so compare them at one standard error from the maximum, where each parabola has dropped by $\frac12$: the cubic term $\frac16\ell_n'''\,\mathrm{SE}^3$ is $\frac16 \times 8.2 \times 0.188^3 = 0.009$ in $\theta$ and $\frac16 \times 1{,}371 \times 0.043^3 = 0.018$ in $p$, twice as large, so the parabola fits better in $\theta$. (b) $\hat{\theta}^2/\widehat{\var}(\hat{\theta}) = 0.598^2/0.188^2 = 10.1$, against $11.41$ in $p$: the two Wald statistics differ, as \cref{ch:wald-lrt} said they would, and the likelihood ratio statistic $10.60$, which does not depend on the scale, sits between them.
\end{solution}
